# Directing the Machine
## A Course in Creative Authorship for the AI-Augmented World


## Warning

This works best as a **reference manual and meta-curriculum** for a highly motivated, already-disciplined practitioner with time, external feedback, and real stakes. It works poorly as a universal self-directed course for someone who needs momentum, simplicity, and a reliable feedback loop.

---

## What works

**1. The central diagnosis is correct.**  
AI makes production cheaper. It does not make purpose, taste, problem-selection, or responsibility cheaper. The course’s shift from “prompting” to “authorship” is exactly right.

**2. The pedagogical spine is strong.**  
Scaffolding removal, transfer tests, problem-finding, positionality, non-instrumental attention, play, calibration, and the Blank-Sheet Test form a coherent progression. The course does not just add AI to an old curriculum; it redesigns around judgment.

**3. It distinguishes execution-as-production from execution-as-knowledge.**  
This is one of the best sections. It avoids both AI utopianism (“craft no longer matters”) and AI denialism (“only handwork counts”). It asks you to understand what craft gives you so you can judge what the machine produces.

**4. Outcome quality vs decision quality is a real contribution.**  
Most creative courses collapse these. This one separates them into quadrants: lucky success, mastery, learning-in-progress, and failure. That prevents rationalisation and anti-intellectualism.

**5. Calibration and range are handled unusually well.**  
The course does not just ask “were you right?” It asks whether you were as right as you expected, and whether your calibration has become narrowness. That is a sophisticated distinction.

**6. Failure protocols are differentiated.**  
Output failure, habit failure, motivational failure, and log decay are not the same problem. Treating them separately is mature and practical.

**7. It takes ethics, refusal, positionality, and professional practice seriously.**  
These are not bolted on. They are part of authorship.

---

## What doesn’t work

**1. It is massively overbuilt.**  
The document contradicts its own best advice. It says “do not read the whole course before beginning,” then presents hundreds of pages of architecture, appendices, templates, and protocols. A learner can spend weeks setting up logs and still not have made anything.

**2. Self-assessment is unreliable without external calibration.**  
The course knows this and includes blind self-assessment, calibration networks, and critique protocols. But it assumes you can build a network. Many people cannot, or will build a polite one. Without honest external eyes, the mastery gates become self-congratulatory.

**3. The workload is unrealistic for most people.**  
18–25 hours/week for 12 weeks, plus logs, plus deepening, is a full-time unpaid programme. Appendix O acknowledges this, but the Core spine still assumes significant time. Many learners will fail, then misdiagnose it as a discipline problem.

**4. Logging can become productivity theatre.**  
The course warns about log decay, but its solution is often another field, another audit, another protocol. For many people, the logs will become the work. The ratio of thinking-about-practice to practice will invert.

**5. Calibration percentages risk false precision.**  
Predicting tool behaviour is one thing. Assigning 75% confidence to whether a poem “works” is another. Creative judgment is often retrospective, contextual, and embodied. The calibration log is useful for systems, less useful for taste.

**6. Problem-finding and positionality can become paralysis.**  
The course includes the reciprocal principle—“a question is not exploration until it changes what you do”—but the apparatus still encourages endless framing. Positionality can also become a purity test: “Do I have standing?” is a good question, but no one has full standing. It should be a compass, not a gate.

**7. It privileges verbal, analytical, documentation-heavy modes.**  
The course admits this. But if your practice is primarily tacit, visual, sonic, or embodied, the course may pathologise your process. It can make you feel you must narrate everything to be legitimate.

**8. It cannot replace community, scene, or real stakes.**  
It says so. But those are not optional extras. They are often where creative judgment actually develops. A self-directed course without a scene can plateau quietly.

**9. The mastery gates are subjective.**  
“Can you defend this?” “Is this mine?” “Would I still know how to begin?” These are good questions. They are not reliable assessment criteria without an external examiner. You can pass yourself.

---

## Warnings for someone following this course

**1. Do not read the whole document first.**  
Start with Module 0, Week 0, and the weekly loop. Treat everything else as reference. If you read it all before making anything, you have already failed the first transfer test.

**2. Do not let the logs replace the making.**  
Set a hard cap: 10 minutes of logging per session. If you spend more time designing your Notion system than drawing, writing, or building, stop and simplify.

**3. Get one honest external critic early.**  
Not a fan, not a polite friend. Someone who will tell you when your work is generic, self-indulgent, or unclear. If you cannot find one, pay for a monthly critique or join a serious workshop. The course’s self-assessment protocols are not enough alone.

**4. Treat the workload honestly.**  
If you work full-time, do not attempt the full-time intensive. Use the distributed path. Burnout is not a character flaw; it is an intensity mismatch.

**5. Beware intellectualisation.**  
Problem-finding is not the same as making. A beautiful problem-finding log can be procrastination. Commit to something small and finish it. The course says this; you must actually do it.

**6. Beware calibration narrowness.**  
High calibration can mean you only attempt what you can already predict. Deliberately make something you do not know how to evaluate. Add anchors that surprise you. Demote things you loved for bad reasons.

**7. Use positionality as honesty, not purity.**  
You will never have perfect standing. The question is whether you are speaking from, with, for, about, or over. Name it, stay accountable, and do not let fear stop every attempt. But do not extract from communities you have no relationship to.

**8. Do not force a position statement.**  
Authorship is a pattern of commitments visible in the work, not a manifesto you write in Week 11. Let the work reveal the pattern. If the written position and the inferred position differ, that gap is useful evidence—not a failure.

**9. Adapt if you are not primarily verbal.**  
If you are a visual, sonic, or embodied practitioner, do not let the course’s analytical bias make you feel illegitimate. Use the parts that help. Ignore the parts that don’t. The goal is your practice, not fidelity to the document.

**10. Know when to stop.**  
The course gives protocols for stopping, abandoning, and finishing. Use them. Endless revision is not rigor. A finished mediocre work teaches more than a perpetually unfinished brilliant one.

**11. Be careful with disclosure and legal issues.**  
The course says disclosure is contextual. That is true, but in professional work, contracts and laws matter. Do not assume. For client work, get legal advice. Do not upload confidential material to tools that train on inputs.

**12. The course cannot give you drive, community, or a career.**  
It says this. Believe it. It can structure your practice. It cannot make you want to make things. It cannot give you a scene. It cannot make you professional. Those are separate projects.

---

## Bottom line

Use the **Core spine**: problem-finding → craft study → concept → project → transfer → critique/reflection. Keep the problem-finding log, practice log, and decision log. Do the non-instrumental attention and play sessions. Get external feedback. Then ignore most of the appendices until you need them.

The course’s best line is also its real test:

> **You are allowed to begin without permission. You are allowed to make something nobody asked for. You are allowed to take it seriously.**

But the warning is equally simple: **the document is not the practice. The practice is the practice.** If you spend more time managing the course than making things, you have already lost the thread.

> *Can you encounter an unfamiliar creative problem, determine what matters, formulate a position, build a process, judge the results, and make something worth defending — without being told what the exercise is?*

> [!important] Course Goal
> *When nobody asks you to make anything at all, can you find a problem worth pursuing — and can you take responsibility for the decision to pursue it?*
>
> Every part of this course prepares you for this question. Transfer tests remove scaffolding. Independent practice removes the mentor. The Blank-Sheet Test removes the exercise. But the ultimate test removes the **assignment itself**. It asks whether you can generate a problem where none was given, and then own the consequences of pursuing it.

> [!important] Do not read the whole course before beginning

### The Weekly Practice Loop

Each week, your practice follows this sequence:

| Phase | Time | Action |
|---|---:|---|
| **Read** | 20–30 min | One core concept from the week's module |
| **Practise** | 60–90 min | One deliberate practice drill |
| **Study** | 60 min | One craft study (visual or writing) |
| **Make** | 2–3 hrs | Project application |
| **Diagnose** | 30 min | Diagnose against the brief |
| **Log** | 15 min | Practice log + decision log + problem-finding log |
| **Transfer** | 30–60 min | One transfer rep |
| **Play** | 30–60 min | Optional: unstructured making for its own sake |

Everything else in this document is **reference material**. Consult it when you need it, not before.

### Core vs Deepening

The course is deliberately rich. Not every learner needs every element in every week.

**Core** — non-negotiable. Weekly spine:
> **Problem-finding → one craft study → one core concept → one project application → one transfer test → one critique/reflection**

**Deepening** — optional depth. Choose based on your baseline, available hours, and current phase focus:
> Additional practice drills · extended study protocols · supplementary anchor work · cross-medium synthesis · public process · third-mode exploration

Core keeps you moving. Deepening makes you distinctive. Do not confuse them.

# Module 0 — How to Practice This Course

[[#Table of Contents|↑ Back to TOC]]

> [!important] The Missing Layer
> This course trains judgment and authorship. But judgment does not improve from project work alone. It improves through **deliberate practice** — isolated, repeated, feedback-rich training of specific sub-skills.

## Goal

Learn to design practice rather than only complete assignments.

## Core Distinction

> [!warning] Practice is not production
> **Production** aims at a deliverable. **Practice** aims at changing your reliable capacity. A practice session may produce nothing worth keeping. That is not failure. That is the point.

## The Deliberate Practice Cycle

Every practice session follows this loop:

1. **Name one micro-skill.** Not "get better at composition" — but "place the focal point so the eye enters at the intended location."
2. **Define an observable success criterion.** Something you can check without ambiguity.
3. **Set difficulty just beyond current reliability.** You should fail roughly 2–4 reps out of 10.
4. **Do focused reps.** Short, repeated, isolated from the full project.
5. **Get immediate feedback.** Self-diagnosis against the criterion, or external eyes.
6. **Correct one variable.**
7. **Re-test.**
8. **Space and interleave** with other skills.
9. **Log what changed.**
10. **Transfer to a new context.**

## Practice Session Template

| Phase | Time | Action | Output |
|---|---:|---|---|
| Intention | 5 min | Name micro-skill + criterion | Practice card |
| Model | 10 min | Study anchor / demo | Mechanism notes |
| Drill | 20–40 min | Focused reps | Practice artifacts |
| Feedback | 10 min | Diagnose against criterion | Error list |
| Correction | 10 min | Redo one rep | Improved rep |
| Log | 5 min | Record | Practice log entry |

## Difficulty Regulation

> [!tip] The 70–80% Rule
> If you succeed 9/10 reps, increase difficulty.
> If you fail 6/10 reps, reduce difficulty or isolate a smaller skill.
> The productive zone is roughly 70–80% success — enough failure to learn, enough success to reinforce.

## Practice Log Fields

Every practice session records:

- Date
- Micro-skill
- Success criterion
- Reps completed
- Difficulty level (1–5)
- Errors observed
- Correction applied
- Result after correction
- Next session focus

> [!note] Logging Should Serve Practice, Not Replace It
> If you spend more time logging than practising, you have the ratio wrong. One line per field is enough. The log is a thinking tool, not a compliance document.

## Weekly Minimum (Core)

| Activity | Frequency | Tier |
|---|---|---|
| Deliberate practice blocks (60–90 min) | 3× | Core |
| Interleaving session (mix visual, writing, pipeline) | 1× | Core |
| Transfer rep (remove one scaffold) | 1× | Core |
| Non-instrumental attention session | 1× | Core |
| Play session (unstructured making) | 1× | Core |
| Rest / consolidation (no generation) | 1× | Core |

> [!note] Why Interleaving Matters
> Blocked practice feels more productive. Interleaved practice produces better transfer. Mixing visual, writing, and pipeline skills in one session forces your brain to select the right approach rather than simply repeat a motion.

## Play Practice

> [!important] Why Play Is Core
> A course this structured can accidentally train rigidity. Play is the counter-practice. It is not optional. It is the space where you encounter the unexpected, the silly, the joyful, and the genuinely new.

### What Counts as Play

Play is **unstructured making for its own sake**. It has:

- no brief
- no deliverable
- no diagnosis
- no criteria
- no audience

It may produce something. It may produce nothing. Both are fine.

### Examples

- Drawing badly on purpose
- Writing nonsense
- Combining media that don't belong together
- Making something with no intention of showing it
- Following a stupid idea to see where it goes
- Breaking a tool to see what happens
- Making something that would embarrass you if anyone saw it

### Rules

- No evaluation
- No logging (except perhaps one line: "What did I enjoy?")
- No sharing unless you want to
- No converting it into a project

### Why This Matters

> [!important] Play is not a reward for finishing work. It is an input to the work.
> The material that surfaces from play often becomes the material that makes the work distinctive. Without play, the practice narrows to what you already know how to do.

### Log

After play, write one line:

> What did I enjoy?

Do not analyse it. Do not integrate it. Just record it.

# The Frame Question

[[#Table of Contents|↑ Back to TOC]]

> [!question] The Governing Question
> **Can you encounter an unfamiliar creative problem, determine what matters, formulate a position, build a process, judge the results, and make something worth defending — without being told what the exercise is?**

This question governs the entire course.

It changes what counts as progress.

You have not mastered a concept because you can repeat its terminology. You have not mastered a pipeline because you can reproduce a workflow. You have not developed taste because you can identify a preferred image. You have not demonstrated authorship because you can write an artist statement.

Mastery appears when the scaffolding is removed.

The course therefore moves deliberately from **high structure to lower structure**:

> [!important] Progression
> **given problem → ambiguous problem → self-defined problem → unfamiliar problem → no problem supplied at all.**

The early exercises teach you *how to think*. The later exercises test whether the thinking survives when nobody tells you what to think about.

That is the central design principle.

# The Course Goal

[[#Table of Contents|↑ Back to TOC]]

> [!question] The Ultimate Goal
> **When nobody asks you to make anything at all, can you find a problem worth pursuing — and can you take responsibility for the decision to pursue it?**

This goal sits above every other in the course.

It is not tested by a single exercise. It is tested by how you behave when no exercise is given.

## The Three Layers of Mastery

| Layer                | Question                                                                | Where it is tested                          |
| -------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------- |
| **1 — Capability**   | Can I do the work?                                                      | Parts I–V                                   |
| **2 — Independence** | Can I determine what work needs to be done?                             | Part VI + Independent Practice              |
| **3 — Agency**       | Can I decide what is worth doing, and take responsibility for doing it? | Continuing Practice + the rest of your life |

Layer 1 is what most courses teach. Layer 2 is what most professional programmes aim for. Layer 3 is what this course is actually about.

A practitioner who can produce excellent work on demand but cannot determine what work needs to be done has achieved capability without independence.

A practitioner who can determine what work needs to be done but cannot decide what is worth doing, or own the consequences of that decision, has achieved independence without agency.

The course is complete only when all three layers are present.

## How the Course Prepares for It

Every structural feature of the course is designed to lead here:

| Feature | What it removes |
|---|---|
| Transfer tests | The rehearsed context |
| Part VI | The scaffolded premise |
| Independent Practice | The external mentor |
| The Blank-Sheet Test | The exercise itself |
| The Monthly Problem-Finding Session | The supplied problem |
| The Unassigned Beginning | The predetermined medium |
| The Annual New-Domain Transfer | The familiar territory |

By Month 9, you should be able to locate, select, own, and defend a problem that nobody handed you.

## Why Responsibility Is Part of the Goal

The course repeatedly emphasises defense, refusal, ethics, provenance, and client responsibility. That is because ==authorship is not merely the ability to originate — it is the willingness to own the consequences of what you originate.==

A practitioner who can generate problems but cannot stand behind them has not completed the course.

A practitioner who can generate problems *and* take responsibility for their selection, their framing, their execution, and their effects has.

# The Three Layers of Mastery

[[#Table of Contents|↑ Back to TOC]]

The course is structured around three progressive layers. Each is necessary. None is sufficient alone.

## Layer 1 — Capability

> **Can I do the work?**

This is the layer most creative training addresses. It concerns whether you have the skills, methods, and internal knowledge to produce work at a competent level.

Capability is necessary. It is not the point.

## Layer 2 — Independence

> **Can I determine what work needs to be done?**

This layer concerns whether you can define the problem, choose the medium, construct the process, and set the criteria — without external structure supplying those decisions.

It is tested by transfer tests, the Blank-Sheet Test, and independent practice.

Independence is the difference between being trained and being educated. It is not "doing without structure." It is **supplying your own structure**.

## Layer 3 — Agency

> **Can I decide what is worth doing, and take responsibility for doing it?**

This layer concerns whether you can originate a direction, choose a problem, own the consequences — not only when assigned, but when nothing is asked of you at all.

Agency is what the ultimate goal tests.

## Why the Layers Matter

| Layer | Failure mode if absent |
|---|---|
| Capability | You have nothing to direct. |
| Independence | You can only work on rehearsed problems or externally defined ones. |
| Agency | You can produce, but you cannot decide what is worth producing, and you cannot own what you produce. |

Most AI-creative courses stop at Layer 1. This one is designed to reach Layer 3.

# The Thesis

[[#Table of Contents|↑ Back to TOC]]

> [!abstract] Thesis
> **Creative authorship in an AI-augmented pipeline is the ability to find, notice, choose, specify, build, judge, revise, and stand behind a course of action.**

AI changes the economics of production. It can make producing variants, drafts, images, layouts, research summaries, edits, and transformations much faster and cheaper. That does not remove the need for creative judgment. It changes where the bottleneck appears.

The course therefore does not define the human contribution as "doing everything by hand." Nor does it define the human contribution as merely "having ideas."

It trains a complete loop:

> [!important] The Loop
> **Find → Observe → Premise → Specify → Build → Diagnose → Curate → Direct → Defend → Transfer**

The goal is not to become a better prompter.

The goal is to become a person who can use prompts, models, tools, drawings, writing, references, experiments, and other people as components of an authored process without surrendering authorship to any of them.

The final test is not whether you can control a machine.

It is whether, when confronted with a problem the course did not prescribe — or a situation where no problem has been named — you can decide what the problem actually is, and take responsibility for that decision.

# What This Course Actually Trains

[[#Table of Contents|↑ Back to TOC]]

The course develops **eleven Core competencies**, four **Contextual competencies**, and one final meta-competency.

> [!important] Why the Distinction
> The course is aimed at **independent creative authorship**. Not every practitioner will work collaboratively, teach, publish process, or work professionally as a freelancer. The Core competencies are universal. The Contextual competencies apply when your practice involves them — and when it does, they are not optional.

## Core Competencies

### 1. Problem-Finding

Can you locate a problem worth pursuing before anyone tells you what the problem is?

This sits upstream of observation. Observation notices what is present. Problem-finding decides what among the noticed deserves sustained attention.

> [!important] Definition
> **Problem** — a meaningful unresolved condition worth changing, investigating, revealing, reframing, or exploring.
>
> **Problem-finding** — the ability to identify such a condition and decide whether it warrants sustained attention.

A problem may be pursued through any of several modes:

| Mode | Question |
|---|---|
| Solve | How do I fix this? |
| Investigate | What is actually happening here? |
| Reveal | What is hidden that should be seen? |
| Reframe | How else could this be understood? |
| Explore | Where does this lead if I follow it? |
| Construct | What question should I be asking? |

The course treats problem-finding as a trainable faculty, not a mysterious gift.

> [!important] The Final Goal Is Whether You Can Do This When Nothing Is Assigned.
> See [[#Module 2 — Problem-Finding|Module 2]] and [[#The Monthly Problem-Finding Session|The Monthly Problem-Finding Session]].

### 2. Observation

Can you see what is actually present before imposing a story about it?

For visual work this includes perceptual analysis: shape, mass, proportion, spatial relationships, gesture, value grouping, edge hierarchy, rhythm, and material behaviour.

For writing this includes reading attentively enough to notice claim, structure, voice, rhythm, register, omission, repetition, implication, and rhetorical effect.

For time-based and spatial work this includes noticing duration, pacing, sequence, volume, silence, and the way a body or object occupies space over time.

> [!important] Observation Is Upstream of Diagnosis. You Cannot Diagnose What You Have Not Learned to Perceive.

### 3. Premise

Can you move from what you notice to what is worth making?

A premise is not a subject. It is a proposition that creates a reason for the work to exist.

You learn to move from:

> observation → question → tension → premise → intention.

This is the course's answer to the missing upstream problem in many AI workflows:

> [!important] The Machine Can Help Generate Possibilities, But the Practitioner Must Decide Which Possibility Is Worth Pursuing.

### 4. Specification

Can you turn intention into constraints, references, criteria, risks, and rejection conditions precise enough for action?

Specification converts judgment into an operational form.

### 5. Diagnosis

Can you identify the highest-level unresolved problem rather than the most visible error?

You learn to move from:

> symptom → level → cause → decision → test.

### 6. Curation

Can you establish and maintain criteria across many alternatives?

You learn to use reference classes, anchors, comparative judgment, explicit criteria, and demotion rather than relying on isolated preference.

### 7. Systems

Can you build a repeatable process whose important variables are understood?

A pipeline is not valuable merely because it works once. You must understand what changes the result, what breaks the system, and how to recover it.

### 8. Prediction / Causal Calibration

Can you anticipate what will happen before you run the experiment — and how confident are you?

There is a difference between knowing what happened and knowing what was likely to happen beforehand. The latter demonstrates an internal model of the system. Calibrated prediction is one of the clearest indicators that you actually understand a workflow, a medium, or an audience.

> [!important] Calibration, Not Correctness
> The useful question is not "Was I right?" It is "Was I as right as I expected to be?"

This is a **universal loop** across the course, not a Part III-only skill. It applies to:

- composition and formal choices
- writing and voice
- audience response
- client decisions
- curation
- tool behaviour
- collaboration dynamics
- problem selection itself

### 9. Direction

Can you make consequential decisions under constraints imposed by audience, client, deadline, medium, ethics, budget, collaborators, and imperfect information?

Direction introduces reality.

### 10. Authorship

Can you state what the work is for, what you believe, what you refuse, what you consistently pursue, and why?

Authorship is not a mood or aesthetic signature. It is a pattern of repeated commitments — including the commitment to take responsibility for what you choose to pursue.

### 11. Transfer

Can you do all of the above when the exercise itself is not supplied?

This is the highest-order competency.

The course therefore treats **Transfer** as the final criterion rather than merely one item in a rubric.

## Contextual Competencies

These apply when your practice involves them. When it does, they are not optional.

### 12. Collaboration

Can you share authorship with an equal — negotiating decision rights, resolving taste conflicts, splitting economics, and preserving the integrity of both positions — without defaulting to deference or dominance?

Required when: you work with peers as co-creators rather than clients or critics.

See [[#Module 14 — Collaborative Direction|Module 14]].

### 13. Transmission

Can you scaffold someone else's problem-finding, premise generation, or diagnostic vision without doing the work for them?

> [!important] Transmission Is Not the Final Test
> Teaching is valuable evidence of internalisation, but it is **not necessary for creative agency**. A person can be an exceptional artist, writer, filmmaker, or independent researcher without teaching anyone.
>
> **Transmission is a high-order test of whether the framework has become portable knowledge. It is not a prerequisite for authorship.**

Required when: you mentor, teach, lead a team, or collaborate with less experienced practitioners.

See [[#Module 19 — Teaching the Framework|Module 19]].

### 14. Public Process

Can you decide which internal artifacts become external work, and shape them for a public audience?

Required when: your practice is pedagogical, critical, documentation-oriented, or public-facing.

See [[#Module 18 — Public Process|Module 18]].

### 15. Professional Practice

Can you operate as a practitioner inside contracts, scope, pricing, client relationships, and ethical disclosure?

Required when: you work with clients, stakeholders, or in any paid capacity.

See [[#Module 13 — Professional Practice Begins|Module 13]] and [[#Appendix M — Professional Practice Reference|Appendix M]].

## Meta-Competency

### 16. Agency

Can you do all of the above when nothing is assigned, and take responsibility for the decisions you make?

Agency sits above every other competency. It is what the ultimate goal tests.

# The Core Distinction — Execution vs Creative Knowledge

[[#Table of Contents|↑ Back to TOC]]

> [!important] Foundational Principle
> **Automate production where useful. Preserve and deliberately train the knowledge that production gives you.**
>
> This principle sits alongside the frame question as one of the course's two foundations. Without it, the course risks becoming prompt literacy for people who do not understand the medium.

> [!important] Two Meanings of Execution
> The distinction matters because the course asks you to automate one and deliberately train the other.

## Execution-as-Production

The mechanical production of a finished artifact: rendering, drafting, formatting, resizing, compositing, generating variants, correcting surface errors, and other operations that can increasingly be delegated to software or models.

This layer is becoming more automatable.

## Execution-as-Knowledge

The understanding acquired through actual making.

A person who has drawn figures understands balance, weight, construction, proportion, foreshortening, and gesture differently from someone who only knows those words.

A person who has written hundreds of pages understands pacing, sentence pressure, revision, omission, voice, and reader attention differently from someone who can ask a model to "make the prose tighter."

A person who has cut a sequence understands rhythm, duration, and the weight of a held frame differently from someone who has only described those concepts.

This course therefore does **not** ask you to abandon craft.

It asks you to understand what craft is doing.

For visual students, observational and constructional study remains part of the course.

For writing students, close reading, imitation, drafting, revision, and editing remain part of the course.

For students working in time-based, spatial, or performance modes, durational and embodied study remains part of the course.

The aim is not to compete with AI at handwork. The aim is to make sure you have enough embodied and analytical knowledge to judge what the machine produces.

# Scaffolding Removal as Pedagogical Principle

[[#Table of Contents|↑ Back to TOC]]

> [!important] Formal Pedagogical Concept
> **Scaffolding removal** is the course's central mechanism for producing independence. Every major transition removes a specific form of external structure. The removal is deliberate and sequenced. Scaffolds may be reintroduced deliberately when they serve the work, but the practitioner must remain capable without them.

## The Scaffolding Sequence

| Stage | What is supplied | What is removed |
|---|---|---|
| Part I | Exercise, ladder, template, references | — |
| Part II | Exercise, template | Ladder |
| Part III | Exercise | Template |
| Part IV | Category | Exercise |
| Part V | Situation | Category |
| Part VI | Conditions only | Situation |
| Month 4 | Brief | Conditions |
| Month 5 | Category | Brief |
| Month 6 | Theme | Category |
| Month 7 | Real-world situation | Theme |
| Month 8 | Nothing | All external input |
| Month 9 | Nothing | Even the problem itself |

## Scaffolding vs Structure

> [!important] The Distinction
> The goal is not for Month 9 to become *"Here's a blank page. Good luck."*
>
> The goal is: **the learner supplies the structure.**

The real progression is:

> **external structure → internalised structure → self-authored structure**

A mature practitioner is usually **more structured internally**, not less.

Independence does not mean refusing templates, briefs, or tools. It means not being dependent on them. A professional can pick up a scaffold deliberately when it is efficient, and set it down when it is not.

## What Scaffolding Removal Is Not

It is not abandonment. Each removal is preceded by practice with the scaffold present. The scaffold is removed only after the underlying capacity has been trained.

It is also not a test of grit. If the removal produces collapse, that is diagnostic — it tells you what to practise next, not what to give up on.

## Why It Matters

> [!question] The Decisive Question
> **If the course disappeared tomorrow, would I still know how to begin?**

A practitioner trained with permanent scaffolding can only work within scaffolds. A practitioner trained through scaffolding removal can work anywhere.

The removal is the course.

# The Six-Month Independent Practice

[[#Table of Contents|↑ Back to TOC]]

| Month | Scaffold source | Your responsibility |
|---|---|---|

> [!tip] Where Scaffolding Comes From Without an Instructor
> - **Real opportunities**: freelance briefs, open calls, commissions, collaborations.
> - **Peer-issued challenges**: someone in your calibration network writes a brief for you.
> - **Public commitments**: announcing a project and a deadline publicly.
> - **Self-imposed constraints**: you write the brief, then follow it without modification.
> - **External stakeholders**: real or simulated audiences who care about the outcome.
> - **Problem-finding sessions**: the recurring practice that keeps the upstream faculty alive.

# Self-Assessment Framework

[[#Table of Contents|↑ Back to TOC]]

> [!important] No Instructor Means You Must Become a Reliable Assessor of Your Own Work
> This is harder than it sounds. The protocols below are designed to reduce self-deception.

## The Principle of Assessment

Assess the **quality of the thinking loop**, not only the artifact.

Output quality still matters. A professional cannot indefinitely produce weak work while writing excellent rationales. But output is evaluated together with the decisions that produced it.

The assessment therefore asks four questions:

1. **Did I see accurately?**
2. **Did I make meaningful decisions?**
3. **Did I learn from the result?**
4. **Could I repeat the reasoning under changed conditions?**

## Primary Evidence

Every substantive exercise generates some combination of:

- problem-finding log
- premise or intention
- brief
- references
- acceptance criteria
- rejected alternatives
- diagnosis
- decision log
- revised output
- reflection
- transfer test
- practice log entries

> [!tip] Reduce the Documentation Load
> You do not need every log for every project. Choose the ones that serve the work. At minimum, keep:
> - Problem-finding log (always)
> - Practice log (always)
> - Decision log (for major projects)
> - Calibration log (for system changes)
>
> Everything else is optional. The practice is the point.

## Secondary Assessment — Live Defense

From Week 4 onward, selected projects are defended live — to a peer, a collaborator, or yourself on camera.

You must explain:

- what the work is trying to do
- what you decided first
- what you rejected
- where the system failed
- what you changed
- what external feedback changed your mind
- what you refused to change and why
- what problem you chose to pursue and why it mattered

> [!tip] Self-Defense Protocol
> Record yourself answering these questions. Wait 24 hours. Watch the recording. Note where you hesitated, where you were vague, and where you were defending rather than explaining. That hesitation is diagnostic.

## Mastery Scale

| Score | Label | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| **4** | Transferable | Works independently in an unfamiliar context and can explain or teach the principle |
| **3** | Functional | Uses the framework reliably in familiar contexts |
| **2** | Emerging | Understands the concept but applies it inconsistently |
| **1** | Not Yet | Cannot reliably identify or apply the principle |

## Progression Rule

You normally progress at **3+**, with no critical criterion at 1.

A Part VI or independent-practice transfer gate requires evidence of **independent transfer**, not merely a 3 on a rehearsed exercise.

> [!warning] Self-Assessment Calibration
> Every 4 weeks, compare your self-assessment against external evidence: peer feedback, public response, whether the work achieved its stated intention, and whether you can reproduce the result. If your self-scores are consistently higher than external evidence suggests, you have a calibration problem. If consistently lower, you have a confidence problem. Both are diagnosable and fixable.

## Blind Self-Assessment Protocol

1. Complete a project.
2. Wait 1 week.
3. Return to the work with no memory of your intentions.
4. Score it against the rubric as if it were someone else's.
5. Compare the blind score against your in-the-moment score.
6. Investigate the gap.

# Outcome Quality vs Decision Quality

[[#Table of Contents|↑ Back to TOC]]

> [!important] An Unresolved Tension
> The course says output quality matters. But much of the assessment apparatus measures the quality of reasoning about the work. This creates a failure mode: a practitioner produces mediocre work while building increasingly sophisticated explanations for why it is conceptually justified.
>
> To resolve this, assessment must separate two axes.

## The Two Axes

**Outcome quality** — how well the artifact achieves its stated intention in its context.

**Decision quality** — how well the practitioner observed, reasoned, specified, diagnosed, curated, and directed.

These are not the same thing. A good process can produce a weak outcome. A weak process can produce a good outcome by luck.

## The Four Quadrants

| | **Weak outcome** | **Strong outcome** |
|---|---|---|
| **Weak reasoning** | **Failure.** The work does not succeed and the process cannot explain why. This is the clearest signal that something needs to change. | **Lucky / unstable success.** The work succeeds but cannot be reliably reproduced. Treat with suspicion. Ask what would happen if the context changed. |
| **Strong reasoning** | **Learning in progress.** The process is sound but the outcome has not yet caught up. This is normal in early phases. Identify which competency is lagging. | **Mastery.** The process is sound and the outcome is strong. This is the target. |

## How to Use the Quadrants

Every substantial project should be scored on both axes.

- If outcome is weak and reasoning is strong: the problem is likely **execution, craft, or time**. Not conceptual.
- If outcome is strong and reasoning is weak: the problem is likely **unstable process or borrowed competence**. Not reproducible.
- If both are weak: the problem is likely **upstream** — problem-finding, premise, or specification.
- If both are strong: the practitioner has demonstrated mastery at this level.

## Why This Matters

Without this distinction, the course is gameable in two directions:

1. **Rationalisation**: producing weak work and defending it with sophisticated reasoning.
2. **Anti-intellectualism**: producing strong work and dismissing process as unnecessary.

Neither is acceptable. The course requires both.

> [!important] Principle
> **A correct decision process does not excuse a consistently ineffective result.**
>
> **An excellent result does not prove an excellent process.**

# The Standing Inquiry

[[#Table of Contents|↑ Back to TOC]]

The course trains creative direction, but it also asks what that capability is for.

> [!question] The Standing Inquiry
> **It is not a course in philosophy. It is a longitudinal record of motive.**

## Weekly

> What did I choose this week that I would choose again if nobody required me to choose it?

One sentence.

## Weekly Practice Question

> What skill did I deliberately train this week, and what evidence shows it changed?

One sentence.

## Weekly Problem-Finding Question

> What problem did I notice this week that nobody asked me to pursue?

One sentence.

## Monthly

> What is this work for, or what is it trying to make possible?

One page.

> [!note] Why "or what is it trying to make possible"
> For artists, "for" can imply instrumental function. Some work is made to discover, articulate, bear witness, inhabit a form, or make a question perceptible. The wider phrasing preserves the existential dimension without collapsing into utility.

## Quarterly

> Has my answer changed? What caused the change?

Compare directly against earlier writing.

## Annually

> What commitments survived the year — including the problems I chose to pursue when nobody assigned them?

Write a synthesis, then identify contradiction rather than forcing consistency.

The purpose is not to manufacture a philosophy. It is to detect drift, borrowed ambition, and the difference between what you say you care about and what your practice repeatedly selects.

# What This Course Cannot Do

[[#Table of Contents|↑ Back to TOC]]

> [!warning] Honest Limits
> This course is ambitious. It is also limited. Knowing its limits will help you use it well.

## It Cannot Manufacture Drive

The course assumes you already have some internal motivation to make things. It can structure that drive, focus it, and train it. It cannot create it from nothing. If you have no desire to make anything when nobody asks, this course will not give you one. It may help you discover whether you have one, but it cannot install it.

## It Cannot Replace Community

The calibration network is a feedback mechanism, not a community. Creative practice is often deeply social — scenes, movements, collaborations, rivalries, mentorships, audiences, institutions. This course treats community as a source of feedback and collaboration as a contextual competency, but it cannot give you a scene. You must find or build one.

## It Cannot Make You Professional

The course trains judgment and authorship. It does not train you to run a business, find clients, negotiate contracts at a professional level, or manage a studio. The professional practice modules are introductions, not full trainings. They point you toward what you need to learn elsewhere.

## It Cannot Guarantee Outcomes

There is no guarantee that completing this course will make you a better artist, writer, or director. It is a structured attempt to train specific faculties. Whether those faculties translate into work that matters — to you or anyone else — depends on factors the course cannot control.

## It Cannot Be All Things

The course is biased toward verbal, analytical, and documentation-heavy modes of working. It privileges articulation, reflection, and explicit reasoning. This suits some practitioners and not others. If your practice is primarily embodied, tacit, or non-verbal, you may need to adapt the course significantly — or find a different one.

> [!tip] Adapt It
> The course is a framework, not a prescription. Change what doesn't serve you. Add what's missing. Ignore what doesn't apply. The goal is your practice, not fidelity to the document.

# Module 1 — The Judgment Gap

[[#Table of Contents|↑ Back to TOC]]

## Goal

Understand what changes when generation becomes cheap, and distinguish production capability from judgment capability.

## Core Ideas

- automation of production is not automation of purpose
- generation is not authorship
- novelty is not significance
- fluency is not clarity
- plausibility is not truth
- beauty is not coherence
- variation is not exploration unless the variation has a question behind it
- **a question is not exploration until it changes what you do**
- a generated choice still has a source, whether human, model-default, dataset convention, or system constraint

> [!important] The Reciprocal Principle
> "Variation is not exploration unless the variation has a question behind it" is necessary but not sufficient.
>
> The reciprocal is also true: **a question is not exploration until it changes what you do.**
>
> Without this, intellectualised problem-finding becomes another form of procrastination. A learner can generate fifty fascinating premises without ever committing to one.

## Procedure — Finding the Human Decision

Take a generated output.

Ask:

1. What is it apparently trying to do?
2. Which decisions are explicit?
3. Which decisions appear to be defaults?
4. Which decision most determines whether it succeeds?
5. Who or what made that decision?
6. What would a human director need to decide next?

Record the result.

> [!example] What This Looks Like in Practice
>
> **Output:** A generated image of a lone figure on a city street at night, neon reflections in puddles.
>
> **What it is apparently trying to do:** Evoke urban loneliness or noir atmosphere.
>
> **Explicit decisions:** The figure's position (center-left), the color palette (cyan/magenta), the reflective surface.
>
> **Apparent defaults:** The cinematic aspect ratio, the shallow depth of field, the "lonely figure in neon city" trope itself. These are model defaults, not chosen.
>
> **Most determining decision:** The choice to center the figure. This makes it a portrait of isolation rather than a portrait of the city.
>
> **Who made it:** The model made it by default. The human did not choose it.
>
> **Next human decision:** Does this image want to be about the figure or about the city? The current composition says figure, but the title and the puddles say city. The human needs to decide which one the work is actually about.

## Constraint Drill

Describe an output without using evaluative adjectives such as good, bad, beautiful, ugly, impressive, weak, strong, polished, or boring.

Then diagnose it.

This separates observation from judgment.

## Transfer Test

Take an unfamiliar output from a medium you do not normally work in.

Identify:

- the likely intention
- the highest-level unresolved problem
- the next decision you would make

No ladder is displayed.

# Module 2 — Problem-Finding

[[#Table of Contents|↑ Back to TOC]]

## Goal

Train the faculty that sits **before** the brief: the ability to locate a problem worth pursuing when nobody has supplied one — and to determine whether you have standing to pursue it.

> [!important] The Missing Upstream Problem
> Most creative training assumes the problem is already given — a brief, a prompt, a theme, a deadline, a client. This course treats problem-finding itself as the highest-order trainable skill, because it is what remains when all external structure is removed.

## Core Distinction

**Problem-solving** — working well within a supplied problem.

**Problem-finding** — deciding which problem is worth pursuing in the first place.

Both matter. But problem-solving without problem-finding produces efficient answers to questions nobody needed asked.

The course trains both, and deliberately weights problem-finding higher as the course progresses.

## Defining "Problem"

> [!important] Broader Than Engineering
> **Problem** — a meaningful unresolved condition worth changing, investigating, revealing, reframing, or exploring.
>
> **Problem-finding** — the ability to identify such a condition and decide whether it warrants sustained attention.

A problem does not have to be solvable to be worth pursuing. It may be:

| Mode | Pursued by |
|---|---|
| Solve | Fixing, resolving, correcting |
| Investigate | Studying, researching, discovering |
| Reveal | Exposing, witnessing, documenting |
| Reframe | Recontextualising, reinterpreting |
| Explore | Following, developing, opening |
| Construct | Building a new question or framework |

This definition is broad enough for visual art, fiction, criticism, illustration, design, research, and essays. It preserves the rigor of problem-finding without importing an instrumental notion of creativity.

## The Problem-Finding Sequence

> [!important] Problem-Finding Sequence
> **Notice → Collect → Frame → Test → Select → Justify**

**Notice** — something in the world, work, or situation draws attention.

**Collect** — gather several such notices without immediately judging them.

**Frame** — articulate each as a possible problem: "What if the issue here is X?"

**Test** — ask of each: is this actually a problem, or is it a topic? Is it worth someone's attention? Is it generative? Do I have standing to pursue it?

**Select** — choose one to pursue, provisionally.

**Justify** — state why this problem, why now, and why you are the one to take it on.

## Positionality — Do I Have Standing?

> [!important] A Problem Worth Pursuing Is Not Automatically a Problem Mine to Pursue
> "Worth pursuing" and "mine to pursue" are different tests. A problem can have tension, stakes, and generativity, and still be one that you would be extracting from a position you do not occupy.

## The Positionality Check

For every candidate problem, ask:

| Question | Purpose |
|---|---|
| Whose story, domain, or lived experience is this problem inside? | Identifies the territory |
| Do I occupy that position, stand adjacent to it, or stand outside it? | Locates you |
| If I stand outside it, what is my relationship to it? | Names the access |
| Am I speaking *about*, *with*, *for*, or *from*? | Distinguishes modes |
| Who is affected by how this problem gets framed? | Identifies stakes-holders |
| Am I extracting, or am I contributing? | Names the exchange |
| What would the people inside this problem say about my taking it on? | The hardest test |
| If I cannot answer that, why not? | Reveals avoidance |

## Positionality Modes

| Mode | You | Appropriate when |
|---|---|---|
| **From** | You are inside the experience | Your own life, work, or community |
| **With** | You are in dialogue with those inside | Collaboration, co-authorship, consent |
| **For** | You are acting in service | Commission, advocacy, explicit request |
| **About** | You are observing from outside | Research, criticism — requires care |
| **Over** | You are speaking as if you were inside when you are not | Never appropriate as a default |

> [!warning] This Is Not a Prohibition
> Positionality is not a rule that says "only make work about yourself." That would be its own failure.
>
> It is a discipline of **knowing where you stand** — so that whatever you make is made with awareness of the relationship between you and the problem, rather than pretending the relationship does not exist.

## Positionality Procedure

For each candidate problem that passes the general tests, run the positionality check.

If you cannot answer the questions honestly, that is diagnostic. It may mean:

- the problem is not yours to pursue
- the problem is yours but you have not thought about why
- the problem is adjacent but requires a collaborator, a consultant, or explicit consent
- the problem is genuinely outside you and should be studied from a critical distance

Record the answer in the problem-finding log.

## What Distinguishes a Real Problem

A real problem is not:

- a topic
- a mood
- a theme
- a genre
- an aesthetic
- a technique in search of an application

A real problem:

- has tension
- has stakes — it matters within some human, intellectual, aesthetic, material, or formal context
- could be pursued in more than one way
- would matter if left unaddressed
- is specific enough to begin
- **can be honestly situated by the person pursuing it**

## Procedure — Problem-Finding Drill

Spend 30–60 minutes with one source of input: a walk, a document, a set of images, a conversation transcript, a dataset, a physical space.

Record at least 5 candidate problems.

For each, write:

| Field | Content |
|---|---|
| Noticed | What drew attention |
| Framed | The problem statement |
| Mode | Solve / Investigate / Reveal / Reframe / Explore / Construct |
| Stakes | Why it matters, and in what domain |
| Positionality | From / With / For / About / Over |
| Standing | Do I have it, and on what basis? |
| Generative | Can it produce multiple premises? |
| Personal | Why me, why now |
| Care | Would I care if nobody saw? |

Select one. State why the others were rejected.

Do **not** produce the work yet.

> [!example] What a Problem-Finding Entry Looks Like
>
> **Source:** A 20-minute walk through a commercial district at dusk.
>
> **Noticed:** Every storefront has a "We're hiring" sign, but all the signs are different — handwritten, printed, formal, desperate, cheerful.
>
> **Framed:** What if the real story of a place is told not by its architecture but by its labor shortage signage?
>
> **Mode:** Reveal
>
> **Stakes:** This is about how communities display (and hide) economic precarity. It matters to anyone who reads a street without knowing what it's saying.
>
> **Positionality:** About. I do not own a business here. I am an observer of a situation I am adjacent to.
>
> **Standing:** Partial. I can document and analyze, but I cannot speak for the business owners. Any work must acknowledge this distance.
>
> **Generative:** Yes — it could become a photo series, an essay, a typographic study, a comparison across neighborhoods.
>
> **Personal:** I noticed it because I walk this route every day and the signs changed. The change is what caught me.
>
> **Care:** Yes. I would want to understand this even if nobody saw it.
>
> **Selected:** Yes — the "labor signage as vernacular portrait" problem.
>
> **Rejected:** Three other problems were too broad ("the aesthetics of late capitalism"), too personal to generalize ("my own job search"), or too familiar ("the death of retail").

## The Problem-Finding Log

Keep a running document titled `Problem-Finding Log`. Every entry records:

- Date
- Source (what you were attending to)
- Noticed
- Framed problems
- Mode
- Stakes
- Positionality
- Standing
- Selected (if any)
- Rejected (and why)
- Follow-up (if pursued)

This log is a longitudinal record of your independent creative attention — including where you decided *not* to pursue a problem because it was not yours.

> [!tip] Keep the Log Light
> One entry per problem-finding session. Three to five candidate problems. One selected. The log should take 10 minutes to write, not 30. If it becomes a burden, simplify it.

## Transfer Test

You are placed in an unfamiliar situation — a space, a document, a dataset, a piece of software — with no instructions.

Within 45 minutes, identify three candidate problems, run the positionality check on each, and defend one as worth pursuing.

No example problem is provided.

## Common Failure Modes

| Failure | Correction |
|---|---|
| Jumping to solution | Stay in problem-finding longer; forbid yourself from proposing solutions |
| Choosing a topic, not a problem | Add tension: what's at stake? |
| Choosing a problem too large | Narrow until you can begin today |
| Choosing a problem because it sounds impressive | Ask: would I care if nobody saw? |
| Choosing nothing | Set a 30-minute timer; produce 5 candidates regardless of quality |
| Optimising prematurely | Return to non-instrumental attention (Module 3) |
| Generating premises endlessly | Apply the reciprocal principle: a question is not exploration until it changes what you do |
| Pursuing a problem you have no standing to pursue | Run the positionality check; if you cannot answer it, do not proceed |
| Refusing every problem because you can never have full standing | No one has full standing; the test is honesty about the relationship, not purity |

# Module 3 — Non-Instrumental Attention

[[#Table of Contents|↑ Back to TOC]]

## Goal

Protect the faculty that produces serendipity: the ability to encounter something you were not looking for.

> [!important] The Counter-Principle
> A course that becomes too explicit about "find a problem → evaluate it → select it" can accidentally train premature optimisation. Not every act of perception should be immediately converted into productivity.

## Core Principle

> [!important] Protect Uninstrumental Attention
> Some observation sessions should have:
> - no problem to solve
> - no output requirement
> - no evaluation
> - no optimisation
>
> Just looking, reading, walking, collecting, sketching, noticing.
>
> The problem-finding process can happen afterward.

## Why This Matters

Creative practice depends on encountering the unexpected. If every observation is immediately converted into a problem to solve or a brief to write, the practitioner loses access to the material that cannot be searched for.

Serendipity is not a luxury. It is an input to the problem-finding process that cannot be generated by the problem-finding process itself.

## Procedure — Non-Instrumental Session

Spend 30–60 minutes in one of the following:

- a walk without a destination
- reading without an objective
- looking at images without evaluating them
- sitting in a public space
- listening to a conversation without recording it
- handling materials without making anything
- listening to sound without analysing it

Do not:

- take notes aimed at a project
- evaluate anything as good or bad
- decide whether anything is useful
- convert any of it into a problem or premise

After the session, do nothing with it.

The material may surface later. Or it may not. Both are acceptable.

## Log

After the session, write one line:

> What did I encounter that I was not looking for?

Do not analyse it. Do not integrate it. Just record it.

Over time, these entries become a record of what your attention does when it is not being directed.

> [!example] What Non-Instrumental Attention Entries Look Like
>
> "The way the light hit the bus stop bench made it look like it was floating."
>
> "I noticed that the sound of the city changes when the wind picks up — it's not louder, it's wider."
>
> "A man was reading a paperback at the laundromat and laughing to himself. I don't know why."
>
> These are not problems. They are not premises. They are just things noticed. Over time, they form a record of what your attention does when nothing is required of it.

## Integration with Problem-Finding

The outputs of non-instrumental attention can feed into problem-finding sessions, but only later. The two practices should remain separate.

> [!important] Sequence
> **Non-instrumental attention → (time passes) → problem-finding session → premise → brief**
>
> Not:
>
> **Non-instrumental attention → immediate problem statement**

## Common Failure Modes

| Failure | Correction |
|---|---|
| Turning the session into research | Stop taking notes; if you must record, use one line |
| Evaluating what you encounter | Return to description |
| Feeling guilty for "wasting time" | The session is the practice |
| Trying to make the session productive | Notice the impulse; do not act on it |
| Skipping the session because it feels unnecessary | It is the most easily sacrificed and the most necessary |

# Module 4 — Diagnostic Vision

[[#Table of Contents|↑ Back to TOC]]

## Goal

Install the principle that diagnosis proceeds from the highest unresolved level downward.

## The Universal Diagnostic Sequence

> [!important] Universal Diagnostic Sequence
> **Intent → Structure → Perceptual / rhetorical effect → Construction / logic → Surface execution**

The exact terms differ by medium, but the principle remains.

## Procedure

1. Encounter the work once without stopping.
2. Record immediate effect separately from diagnosis.
3. State the likely intention.
4. Identify the highest unresolved level.
5. Name the specific decision causing the problem.
6. Propose the smallest high-level intervention that could change the outcome.
7. State what evidence would tell you the intervention worked.

## Important Distinction

> [!warning] Diagnosis Is Not Criticism
> Criticism may describe what is valuable or disappointing.
> Diagnosis asks: **Where is the problem located, what decision produced it, and what change should be tested first?**

> [!example] What a Diagnosis Looks Like
>
> **Work:** A short story opening.
>
> **Immediate effect:** I felt bored by the third paragraph.
>
> **Likely intention:** To establish a sense of quiet dread in a domestic setting.
>
> **Highest unresolved level:** Structure. The story opens with three paragraphs of weather and furniture description before any human action or tension appears.
>
> **Decision causing the problem:** The writer chose to establish setting before establishing stakes.
>
> **Smallest high-level intervention:** Move the paragraph that contains the first hint of conflict (the wife's silence at the breakfast table) to the opening, then let the description follow.
>
> **Evidence that it worked:** A reader would feel the tension before understanding it, rather than waiting for it.

# Module V1 — Visual Observation & Diagnosis

[[#Table of Contents|↑ Back to TOC]]

## Goal

Build visual knowledge that makes visual diagnosis possible.

## Required Observation Studies

Across Weeks 1–2, complete short studies in:

- mass and silhouette
- proportion and relative scale
- gesture and directional force
- perspective and spatial construction
- value grouping
- focal hierarchy
- edge hierarchy
- material differentiation
- environmental relationships

These are not portfolio drawings. They are **perceptual calibration studies**.

## Visual Diagnostic Ladder

1. **Intent** — what should the image do?
2. **Composition** — how does the frame organise attention?
3. **Value / major shape** — does the image read as large masses?
4. **Gesture / action** — what is the directional energy?
5. **Construction** — do objects and figures occupy believable space?
6. **Proportion** — are internal relationships convincing?
7. **Subject-specific structure** — do anatomy / architecture / object systems hold together?
8. **Material** — do surfaces behave coherently?
9. **Rendering** — do edges, colour, detail, and texture support the above?

## Visual Study Protocol

For one reference image:

1. reduce it to three to five major masses
2. identify the focal path
3. identify the dominant directional axis
4. redraw the construction underneath the surface
5. reconstruct the image from memory
6. compare the reconstruction against the reference
7. state what you failed to perceive

Repeat with generated images.

> [!example] What a Visual Perception Study Looks Like
>
> **Reference:** A photograph of a crowded market.
>
> **Step 1 — Major masses:** I reduce it to five shapes: a vertical mass (the crowd), a diagonal mass (the awning), a horizontal mass (the table), a dark mass (shadow under the table), and a light mass (the sky opening).
>
> **Step 2 — Focal path:** My eye enters at the brightest point (the sky opening), moves down the diagonal of the awning, lands on the figure at the table, then spreads into the crowd.
>
> **Step 3 — Directional axis:** The dominant flow is left-to-right, following the crowd's movement.
>
> **Step 4 — Construction:** I redraw the underlying perspective — the vanishing point is slightly left of center, and the table sits on a plane that recedes more steeply than the crowd behind it.
>
> **Step 5 — Memory reconstruction:** I redraw from memory. My version compresses the crowd into a single mass, loses the sky opening, and flattens the table.
>
> **Step 6 — Compare:** The reference has more depth because the sky opening creates a recession. My version is flatter because I didn't perceive the recession.
>
> **Step 7 — What I failed to perceive:** The sky opening is not just brightness — it's a spatial device. It makes the market feel like it's in a courtyard rather than a street.

## Transfer Test

A random image is shown at thumbnail size for five seconds.

Reconstruct:

- dominant mass arrangement
- focal point
- directional flow
- approximate value grouping

Then diagnose where your reconstruction differs from the source.

# Module W1 — Writing Observation & Diagnosis

[[#Table of Contents|↑ Back to TOC]]

## Goal

Build the reading ability required to judge generated prose.

## Writing Diagnostic Ladder

1. **Intent** — what is the piece trying to do?
2. **Claim / premise** — what is it asserting, asking, or proposing?
3. **Argument / structure** — how is the claim developed?
4. **Voice / stance** — who is speaking and from what position?
5. **Rhythm** — how does the prose move?
6. **Sentence / paragraph logic** — do local units cohere?
7. **Clarity** — can the reader follow the thought?
8. **Mechanics** — are the basic conventions working?
9. **Polish** — are surface choices supporting the whole?

## Close-Reading Protocol

For one strong human-written passage:

1. mark where the writer makes a turn
2. mark what is omitted
3. mark where the rhythm changes
4. state what the reader is made to anticipate
5. paraphrase the underlying claim
6. rewrite the passage in neutral prose
7. compare what disappeared

The purpose is to discover that voice is not decoration added after meaning.

> [!example] What a Close Reading Looks Like
>
> **Passage:** The opening of Joan Didion's "The White Album."
>
> **Turn:** The first turn is in the second sentence: "I was supposed to be writing a piece for the *Saturday Evening Post*." The first sentence establishes a scene; the second reveals a task. The reader's expectation shifts from atmosphere to obligation.
>
> **Omission:** Didion never explains what the piece was supposed to be about. The absence is the point — the failure to write it is the subject.
>
> **Rhythm change:** The prose moves from long, observational sentences to short, declarative ones when the narrator's mental state deteriorates. The rhythm mirrors the content.
>
> **Anticipation:** The reader is made to anticipate a breakdown. The opening's calm is ominous because we know Didion's work is often about the failure of calm.
>
> **Underlying claim:** "I was not able to make sense of what was happening, and the attempt to make sense of it is the story."
>
> **Neutral rewrite:** "I was in Los Angeles in 1968. I was supposed to write an article but I couldn't. Many things happened. I couldn't understand them."
>
> **What disappeared:** The tension between the professional obligation and the personal incapacity. The sense that the writing itself is the attempt to hold it together. The voice — the specific way Didion makes you feel her intelligence as a kind of vulnerability.

## Transfer Test

Take a piece in an unfamiliar genre.

Identify the author's likely intention, the strongest structural decision, and one consequential omission without rewriting anything.

# Module 5 — Premise & Intent

[[#Table of Contents|↑ Back to TOC]]

## Goal

Learn to move from observation — or from a found problem — to a reason for the work to exist.

## Core Sequence

> [!important] Premise Sequence
> **Observation / Problem → Question → Tension → Premise → Intention**

## Definitions

**Observation** — something noticed.

**Problem** — a meaningful unresolved condition worth pursuing.

**Question** — something genuinely unresolved.

**Tension** — two forces, ideas, desires, facts, or readings that cannot comfortably coexist.

**Premise** — the proposition the work explores.

**Intention** — what the work is trying to cause in the audience.

## Example

Observation:

> Every person on the train is looking down at a screen.

Question:

> What happens to public space when attention is privately occupied?

Tension:

> A crowded space can feel socially empty.

Premise:

> Depict a public environment in which physical proximity and psychological isolation increase together.

Intention:

> Make the viewer feel the contradiction rather than simply understand it.

## Premise Tests

A viable premise should pass four tests:

1. **Specificity** — it is not simply a topic.
2. **Tension** — there is something to explore or resolve.
3. **Generativity** — it can produce multiple formal or textual decisions.
4. **Commitment** — you can say what you currently believe about it.

## Weak vs Strong Premise

Weak:

> "Loneliness in modern life."

Strong:

> "A person can be surrounded by evidence of belonging while experiencing increasing evidence of irrelevance."

The second can generate choices.

> [!example] Another Pair
>
> **Weak:** "The ethics of AI art."
>
> **Strong:** "A system trained on the labor of millions can produce work that bears no trace of their hands, and the erasure is invisible in the output."
>
> The second has a specific tension (invisible erasure), a specific subject (training data and labor), and a specific formal implication (the work must make the invisible visible).

## Procedure — Premise Generation Drill

Starting from a problem found in Module 2, generate:

- 5 questions
- 3 tensions
- 3 premises
- 2 possible intentions

Do not generate the final work yet.

Choose one premise and explain why the others were rejected.

> [!tip] Rejection Is the Point
> The goal is not to generate three good premises. The goal is to practice the act of choosing between them. The rejection logic is more important than the selected premise. Write one sentence for each rejected premise explaining what it lacked.

## Premise Record

For each premise worth keeping, record:

| Field | Content |
|---|---|
| Observation | What I noticed |
| Problem | The condition worth pursuing |
| Question | What is unresolved |
| Tension | What cannot comfortably coexist |
| Premise | The proposition |
| Intention | What it should cause |
| Mode | Solve / Investigate / Reveal / Reframe / Explore / Construct |
| Positionality | From / With / For / About |
| What I believe | My current position on this |
| Rejected alternatives | What I chose against, and why |

## Transfer Test

You receive an ordinary object, photograph, social situation, or paragraph you did not choose.

Within 30 minutes, develop a premise worth pursuing and explain why it is more than a subject.

# Module 6 — Brief Architecture

[[#Table of Contents|↑ Back to TOC]]

## Goal

Turn a premise into a specification that a human and a production system can act on.

## The Brief Stack

Every brief contains seven layers:

1. **Premise** — why this work exists.
2. **Intention** — what the work should do to its audience.
3. **Constraints** — what is deliberately limited.
4. **References** — what principles are being borrowed or studied.
5. **Acceptance criteria** — what must be true for the work to succeed.
6. **Rejection conditions** — what makes an output unsuitable for revision.
7. **Risks and unknowns** — what you do not yet know.

> [!important] Brief First. Generation Second.
> A brief is not a post-hoc explanation for an output already generated.

## Procedure

1. State the premise in one paragraph.
2. Reduce it to a one-sentence intention.
3. Choose 6–10 constraints.
4. Select 3–5 references and state the principle extracted from each.
5. Write 4–6 acceptance criteria.
6. Write 3 rejection conditions.
7. Name at least 2 genuine uncertainties.
8. Define the first test.
9. Generate or draft.
10. Diagnose against the brief.

## Constraint Experiment

Create three versions of the same brief:

- **open** — three constraints
- **directed** — six constraints
- **severe** — ten constraints

Compare not only output quality but **quality of decisions enabled by the constraints**.

> [!example] Constraint Experiment in Practice
>
> **Premise:** A portrait of a person who is present but not fully available.
>
> **Open brief (3 constraints):** One figure. Interior. Natural light.
>
> **Directed brief (6 constraints):** One figure. Interior. Natural light. The figure is engaged in an activity. The activity is interrupted. No eye contact. The frame is slightly too tight.
>
> **Severe brief (10 constraints):** One figure. Interior. Natural light. The figure is engaged in an activity. The activity is interrupted. No eye contact. The frame is slightly too tight. The figure's hands are visible. There is a second presence implied but not shown. The color palette is limited to two hues. The brightest area is not the figure.
>
> **What the experiment reveals:** The severe brief produces the most interesting results because it forces decisions that the open brief leaves to default. But it also risks overdetermination — the image becomes a puzzle rather than a portrait. The directed brief is often the sweet spot. The point is not that more constraints are better. The point is to feel the difference.

## Acceptance Experiment

Write two briefs for the same premise:

- one with vague criteria
- one with checkable criteria

Generate from both.

Document how the criteria changed curation rather than assuming they improved the artifact.

> [!example] Vague vs Checkable Criteria
>
> **Vague:** "The image should feel lonely."
>
> **Checkable:** "The figure should occupy less than 15% of the frame. The brightest area should be further from the figure than the figure is from the frame edge. The figure's gaze should not meet the viewer's."
>
> The checkable criteria can be verified. The vague criterion cannot. But the checkable criteria do not guarantee loneliness — they guarantee a set of conditions that might produce it. The practitioner still has to judge whether the result works.

## Brief Template

| Layer | Content |
|---|---|
| Premise | |
| Intention | |
| Constraints | |
| References | |
| Acceptance criteria | |
| Rejection conditions | |
| Risks and unknowns | |

## Transfer Test

You receive a premise but not a brief.

Write a complete brief from scratch, then generate from it and diagnose the result against your own criteria.

# Module V2 — Visual Specification

## Goal

## Required Translation

## Thumbnail-First Specification

Before any detailed generation or rendering, produce:

- 6–12 thumbnail compositions
- 3 value schemes
- 2 focal hierarchies
- 2 directional-flow options

> [!important] Composition Must Be Decided Before Detail Begins.

> [!tip] Thumbnails Are Thinking, Not Drawing
> A thumbnail is not a small drawing. It is a decision about where the major masses sit, where the eye enters, and what the value structure does. It can be three lines and a scribble. If it takes more than two minutes, it is not a thumbnail.

## Visual Reference Protocol

For each reference, record:

- what is being borrowed

[[#Table of Contents|↑ Back to TOC]]

## Goal

Begin recording the human contribution at the point where decisions are first made, not retroactively after the work is done.

> [!important] Why Provenance Starts in Part II
> Provenance is usually treated as a delivery concern. It belongs earlier. The first decisions about premise, reference, and constraint already determine what is authored and what is inherited from a tool. Recording them from the start makes the record accurate and the authorship legible.

## What to Record

For every premise and brief produced in Part II, record:

| Field | Content |
|---|---|
| Premise origin | Where did it come from? |
| Human contribution | What did I decide, reject, transform? |
| Tool contribution | What was generated, suggested, or scaffolded? |
| Reference sources | What did I study or borrow principles from? |
| Licence / consent | Any relevant restrictions on reference material |
| Version of tool | Which model or tool, at which point in its evolution? |

## Procedure

1. Open the provenance record template from [[#Appendix L — Operational Templates|Appendix L]].
2. Fill it in at the moment of premise selection.
3. Update it when the brief is written.
4. Revisit it before each diagnosis.

## Why This Matters

> [!important] Provenance Is Not Paperwork
> It is a record of authorship. The question it answers is not only "Where did the material come from?" but **"What did the human actually decide, transform, construct, reject, and contribute?"**

> [!example] What a Provenance Record Looks Like
>
> **Premise origin:** I noticed a pattern on my commute — people holding their phones at increasingly steep angles as if trying to hide them. I asked why. The premise came from that question.
>
> **Human contribution:** I chose the question. I rejected three other interpretations of the pattern. I chose the tension (visibility vs. concealment). I wrote the premise.
>
> **Tool contribution:** None at this stage. The premise was entirely human.
>
> **Reference sources:** None yet. References will be chosen when the brief is written.
>
> **Licence / consent:** N/A at this stage.
>
> **Version of tool:** N/A at this stage.

## The Simplicity Principle

> [!tip] Keep Provenance Light
> The provenance record does not need to be a legal document. It needs to answer one question: what did I actually decide? If you can answer that in a few sentences, the record is complete. The full template in Appendix L is for professional contexts where the record may need to be produced later. For daily practice, a short form is enough.

# Module 8 — Pipeline Thinking

[[#Table of Contents|↑ Back to TOC]]

## Core Idea

A production pipeline is a hypothesis about how decisions become outputs.

Every node, prompt, tool, transformation, reference, or edit should answer:

> [!question] **What variable am I trying to control, and why?**

## Pipeline Layers

1. Brief.
2. Input preparation.
3. Generation or drafting.
4. Structural control.
5. Identity / voice control.
6. Selection.
7. Revision.
8. Verification.
9. Final production.

## Controlled Variation

Change one meaningful variable at a time when learning causality.

Later, deliberately change multiple variables when the goal is production rather than experimentation — but record why.

The course therefore avoids turning "one variable at a time" into a ritual.

> [!example] Controlled Variation in Practice
>
> **System:** A visual pipeline for generating character portraits.
>
> **Variable 1 — Structural conditioning.** I run the pipeline with edge conditioning on, then off. Everything else stays the same.
>
> **Prediction:** With edge conditioning off, the silhouette will drift and the pose will become less controlled.
>
> **Result:** The silhouette drifts, but less than I expected. The pose is still roughly correct. What actually breaks is the consistency of the costume — the model invents new details when the edges are not constrained.
>
> **What I learned:** Edge conditioning is doing more work for costume consistency than for pose. My mental model was wrong. I revise it.
>
> This is the loop: predict, run, compare, revise. One variable at a time. The point is not to be right. The point is to understand the system.

## Variables

For each pipeline, identify:

- inputs
- controllable variables
- hidden variables
- irreversible steps
- expensive steps
- failure points
- recovery points
- quality gates

## Reproducibility

A pipeline is not reproducible simply because the same steps can be rerun.

It is reproducible when reruns reliably satisfy the **same acceptance criteria**, even if the exact artifact differs.

## System Test

Break one variable deliberately.

Predict the failure before running the test.

Then compare prediction against actual behaviour.

This converts tool use into causal understanding.

## Embedded Professional Practice — Tool Discipline and Versioning

> [!important] Professional Practice Begins Here
> Versioning and data policy are not exit concerns. They are part of how you build.

**Versioning:** Use the status-aware naming convention from [[#Appendix L — Operational Templates|Appendix L]]:

`project-name_v01_YYYY-MM-DD_status_purpose`

Status values: `draft` / `review` / `approved` / `final` / `superseded` / `archived`.

**Data policy:** For every tool you use, know:

- what it does with your inputs
- whether it retains them
- whether it trains on them
- what you are permitted to upload
- what happens to your work if the tool changes terms

**Tool refresh discipline:**

| Frequency | Action |
|---|---|
| Monthly | Check for major model updates |
| Quarterly | Test one new tool against your current stack |
| Annually | Re-evaluate your entire tool stack against your practice goals |

> [!tip] Keep Versioning Simple
> The full naming convention is useful for professional work. For daily practice, a simpler system works: `project_v01`, `project_v02`, etc., with a one-line note in a text file explaining what changed. The point is to be able to reconstruct what you did, not to maintain a perfect archive.

# Module 9 — Prediction & Calibration

[[#Table of Contents|↑ Back to TOC]]

## Goal

Develop the ability to anticipate what will happen before running the experiment — and to know how confident you were.

> [!important] Why Calibration Matters
> There is a difference between knowing what happened and knowing what was likely to happen beforehand. The latter demonstrates an internal model of the system. Calibration is one of the clearest indicators that you actually understand a workflow, a medium, or an audience.

> [!important] A Universal Loop, Not a Part III Skill
> **Prediction → outcome → model revision** applies across the entire course: composition, writing, audience response, client decisions, curation, tool behaviour, collaboration dynamics, and even problem selection.
>
> It is introduced here because systems make the loop concrete. It is used everywhere.

## Core Principle

> [!important] Calibration, Not Correctness
> The useful question is not "Was I right?" It is "Was I as right as I expected to be?"
>
> Someone predicting *"I am 90% confident the edge-conditioning change will improve the silhouette"* and being wrong is more informative than someone vaguely saying *"This might help."*

## What Can Be Predicted

| Domain | Example prediction |
|---|---|
| Pipeline | "If I remove the edge conditioning node, the silhouette will drift." |
| Curation | "This work will feel generic to an audience that has seen the reference class." |
| Writing | "This revision will flatten the voice because it removes the qualifying clause." |
| Visual | "The eye will not find the focal point because the value contrast is too low." |
| Audience | "This will read as sincere to one group and ironic to another." |
| Direction | "The client will accept the structure but reject the surface treatment." |
| Collaboration | "My co-director will resist this decision because it changes the shared reference." |
| Problem selection | "If I pursue this problem, I will lose interest within two weeks." |

## Procedure — Calibration Drill

1. Select a system, work, or situation.
2. State what you expect to happen if one variable changes.
3. **State your confidence level** (as a percentage).
4. Record the prediction **before** running the test.
5. Run the test.
6. Compare prediction to outcome.
7. Record direction of error, magnitude of error, and explanation for the mismatch.
8. Revise your internal model.

## Calibration Log

| Field | Entry |
|---|---|
| Date | |
| System | What was being tested |
| Variable changed | |
| Prediction | What I expected |
| Confidence | % confidence |
| Actual | What happened |
| Direction of error | Over- or under-estimated |
| Magnitude of error | How far off |
| Explanation | Why the mismatch |
| Revision | What I now understand |

> [!example] What a Calibration Entry Looks Like
>
> **Date:** 2026-09-14
>
> **System:** Writing pipeline for a personal essay.
>
> **Variable changed:** I removed the qualifier "I think" from the opening claim.
>
> **Prediction:** The voice will become more authoritative, but the essay will feel less honest. I expect this to be a net negative.
>
> **Confidence:** 75%
>
> **Actual:** The voice became more authoritative, but the honesty did not decrease. Instead, the essay became clearer. The qualifier had been hedging, not expressing genuine uncertainty.
>
> **Direction of error:** I over-estimated the negative effect.
>
> **Magnitude:** Moderate. The essay improved more than I expected.
>
> **Explanation:** I confused "honest" with "qualified." They are not the same. The qualifier was a tic, not a commitment.
>
> **Revision:** I now understand that removing qualifiers can increase honesty if the qualifiers were defensive rather than substantive.

## Calibration Metrics

Track these rather than simple hit rate:

- **Calibration curve** — when you say 80%, are you right 80% of the time?
- **Direction of bias** — do you systematically over- or under-estimate?
- **Domain variance** — are you better in some domains than others?
- **Confidence range** — do you only predict when you are near-certain, or do you predict across the whole range?

## The Taste-Narrowness Problem

> [!important] High Calibration and Low Range Can Look Identical on the Log
> If you only generate outputs you already know how to predict, your calibration will be high — and meaningless. You will be predicting well because you have stopped encountering surprise.

## Calibration Range Check

Every quarter, ask:

| Question | Purpose |
|---|---|
| When did I last produce something that surprised me? | Detects range contraction |
| When did I last predict incorrectly in a way that taught me something? | Detects safe prediction |
| Am I generating fewer types of things than I did 3 months ago? | Detects narrowness |
| Is my calibration improving because my model is better, or because my inputs are narrower? | The diagnostic question |

If calibration is high but range is low, the appropriate response is not to celebrate the calibration. It is to **deliberately widen the inputs**:

- pursue an unfamiliar problem
- work in a medium you have not tried recently
- seek critique from someone outside your usual network
- run a reverse-direction test

> [!warning] Calibration and Range Must Be Tracked Together
> A practitioner with high calibration and narrow range is becoming precise at an ever-smaller territory. A practitioner with wide range and poor calibration is producing variety without understanding. The goal is **calibrated range** — accurate prediction across a wide territory.

> [!example] The Narrowing Trap
>
> A writer I know developed an extremely reliable process for producing personal essays in a specific voice. Her calibration was excellent — she could predict with 85% confidence what would work and what wouldn't. But after two years, all her essays sounded the same. She had not noticed that her calibration had become narrowness. She was predicting well because she had stopped trying anything she couldn't predict.
>
> The correction was not to abandon her voice. It was to deliberately write in a form she didn't know how to predict — a play, a critical essay, a series of prose poems — and accept that her calibration would drop. The drop was the point. It meant she was learning again.

## Transfer Test

You are given an unfamiliar workflow or situation.

Before running anything, predict:

- what will happen if one variable changes
- what will break first
- which output will be strongest

Record confidence for each. Then run it and compare.

## Craft Knowledge Is Not Only Correctness

> [!important] Deviation as Craft
> **Craft knowledge is not only the ability to execute conventions; it is the ability to recognise a convention, understand what it accomplishes, and depart from it deliberately.**

This connects craft directly to authorship. Without the second half of this principle, students may learn the course as another correctness framework.

The test is not "Can you do it right?" It is:

> **Do you know what "right" accomplishes, and what breaking it would make possible?**

> [!example] Deviation as Craft
>
> A student learns the convention of the "establishing shot" — a wide shot that orients the viewer in a new location.
>
> **The convention accomplishes:** Spatial orientation. The viewer knows where they are and how the space relates to the action.
>
> **What breaking it makes possible:** If you skip the establishing shot and cut directly to a close-up, the viewer is disoriented. This can be a failure. It can also be a deliberate choice — making the viewer feel the character's disorientation, or refusing the comfort of omniscience.
>
> The practitioner who knows the convention can choose to break it. The practitioner who does not know the convention is just breaking things.

> [!important] The Craft Hours Have a Body Cost
> Deliberate practice (60–90 min blocks, 3× weekly minimum) plus project work plus pipeline iteration produces a real physical load. This is not a mental health concern — it is a repetitive-strain and screen-fatigue concern.

| Practice | Frequency | Purpose |
|---|---|---|

| Screen distance at arm's length or more | Continuous | Reduces eye strain |

### Warning Signs

- persistent wrist, hand, or forearm pain
- numbness or tingling in fingers
- recurring headaches during or after screen work
- eye strain that does not resolve with rest
- shoulder or neck pain that persists across days
- loss of grip strength or fine motor control

> [!warning] These Are Not Signs of Weakness
> They are signs that the body has reached its current capacity. Rest, adjust, and if symptoms persist, seek medical advice. A practice that produces injury is not a sustainable practice.

# Module 10 — Taste as Infrastructure

## Taste Model

> [!important] Usable Taste
> **preference + criteria + reference class + mechanism + purpose**

**Purpose** tells me whether it belongs here.

## Why Purpose Matters

An aesthetically stronger solution can still be the wrong solution for the task. A formally brilliant image that does not serve the premise is not a good image for this project. A beautifully written passage that misrepresents the argument is not a good passage for this piece.

Purpose completes the model by tying taste to fitness.

> [!important] The Question Is Never "Is This Good?" in Isolation.
> It is always "Is this good **for this purpose**?"

> [!example] Taste in Practice
>
> **Preference:** I am drawn to images with high contrast and dramatic lighting.
>
> **Criteria:** I value images where the light source is motivated — it comes from somewhere in the world of the image, not from nowhere.
>
> **Reference class:** This is a portrait. The relevant comparison is not "all images" but "portraits with dramatic lighting."
>
> **Mechanism:** The dramatic lighting works because it creates a clear focal hierarchy. The eye knows where to go.
>
> **Purpose:** The image is for a story about a character who is hiding something. The dramatic lighting serves the purpose because it makes the character partially visible and partially concealed.
>
> **Judgment:** The image is good for this purpose. If the purpose were different — say, a story about a character who is fully seen — the same image would be wrong.

## Comparative Judgment

Whenever possible, compare:

> A vs B vs reference class.

Avoid asking only:

> "Do I like A?"

Instead ask:

- what is A trying to do?
- what does A accomplish better than B?
- by what mechanism?
- under what conditions would B be better?
- does either belong in the current project?

## Slop Criterion

A slop criterion is a named failure mode that causes work to drift toward generic or default behaviour.

Examples:

- generic composition
- generic voice
- ornamental detail without function
- empty symmetry
- forced positivity
- explanatory filler
- stylistic mimicry without structural understanding

The goal is not to ban these forms universally.

The goal is to recognise when they appear without being chosen.

> [!example] What a Slop Criterion Looks Like in Practice
>
> **Slop criterion:** "Faux-profundity."
>
> **What it is:** A sentence or image that gestures at depth without earning it. In writing, it often takes the form of a short, declarative sentence that sounds meaningful but is actually empty — "Everything changed." "She understood." "It was never the same." In visual work, it often takes the form of a solitary figure in a vast landscape, or a close-up of an eye, or hands reaching toward each other without touching.
>
> **When it becomes a problem:** When the work uses these gestures as substitutes for actual specificity. The figure in the vast landscape is not automatically profound. It is profound only if the landscape and the figure have been made specific enough to earn the feeling.
>
> **How to detect it:** Ask: what does this actually mean? If the answer is another abstraction, the gesture is empty. If the answer is a specific image, a specific memory, a specific tension, the gesture has earned its place.

## Demotion

At least three early anchors must be reconsidered and either:

- retained with stronger reasoning
- demoted
- removed

This teaches you that taste is allowed to evolve.

> [!example] What a Demotion Looks Like
>
> **Anchor:** A photograph I added in Week 7 because I loved the composition.
>
> **Why I added it:** The subject was positioned off-center in a way that felt dynamic and unconventional.
>
> **Reconsideration (Week 9):** I look at it again. The off-center composition is dynamic, but it is also arbitrary. The subject could be anywhere in the frame and the image would be the same. The composition is not doing anything specific — it is just unusual.
>
> **Decision:** Demote. Not because the image is bad, but because I confused "unusual" with "meaningful." The composition is a choice the photographer made, but not a choice that serves a purpose I can identify.
>
> **What I learned:** Unusual choices are not automatically good choices. A choice becomes good when it serves a purpose. My criteria were not specific enough.

## Comparative Exercise

Choose two visually attractive images.

Determine which one is actually more useful for the intended purpose.

The point is to separate **attraction** from **fitness**.

## AI-Default Detection

Identify prose that is:

- generic
- over-explanatory
- hedged without reason
- symmetrical for symmetry's sake
- relentlessly helpful
- uniformly polished
- emotionally flattened
- padded with transitions

Again, these are not universal defects. They become problems when they are defaults masquerading as decisions.

> [!example] What AI-Default Prose Looks Like
>
> **Default prose:** "In today's rapidly changing world, it's more important than ever to consider the ways in which technology shapes our understanding of creativity. While there are certainly benefits to these developments, there are also potential drawbacks that warrant careful consideration."
>
> **What makes it default:**
> - "In today's rapidly changing world" — a phrase that has been used so often it no longer means anything
> - "it's more important than ever" — an unearned claim of urgency
> - "the ways in which" — a wordy construction that adds nothing
> - "While there are certainly benefits... there are also potential drawbacks" — a symmetrical structure that says nothing
> - "warrant careful consideration" — a phrase that commits to nothing
>
> **What a non-default version might look like:** "AI tools are changing how we make things. Some of those changes are good. Some are not. I want to look at one specific change — the way these tools make it easier to produce without deciding."
>
> The second version is not more elegant. It is more specific. It commits to a claim. It names a subject. It takes a position. That is the difference.

# Module 11 — Comparative Judgment & Transfer

[[#Table of Contents|↑ Back to TOC]]

## Goal

Move from "having taste" to being able to deploy taste under unfamiliar conditions.

## Reference-Class Method

For every significant judgment:

1. Define what kind of thing this is.
2. Identify at least two strong examples in that class.
3. Compare the work to those examples.
4. Identify the mechanism of difference.
5. Decide whether the difference is desirable **for this purpose**.

> [!example] Reference-Class Method in Practice
>
> **Work:** A short story about a family dinner.
>
> **Reference class:** Domestic realism. Stories that take place in a single setting and derive tension from what is not said.
>
> **Examples in the class:** Carver's "Cathedral." Munro's "The Bear Came Over the Mountain." Chekhov's "The Lady with the Dog."
>
> **Mechanism of difference:** My story uses more dialogue than these examples. The dialogue is doing the work that their narration does — revealing character through what is said rather than through what is observed.
>
> **Is the difference desirable for this purpose?** The purpose is to show a family that cannot communicate directly. The dialogue is not just a stylistic choice — it is the subject. The characters talk past each other. The difference is not just desirable; it is necessary.

## Blind Curation Test

You receive 20 outputs without generation history.

Rank them, then write criteria explaining the ranking.

Only afterward do you learn which were human, generated, edited, or mixed.

> [!important] The Purpose Is to Prevent Provenance from Becoming a Substitute for Judgment.

> [!example] What the Blind Test Reveals
>
> When I ran this test on myself, I ranked a generated image first and a human image last. The generated image was more technically polished — better lighting, cleaner composition. The human image was messier, more ambiguous, and I initially read the ambiguity as a mistake.
>
> After learning the sources, I looked again. The human image was ambiguous because the photographer had made a decision I did not initially understand. The ambiguity was the point. The generated image was polished because the model had made the decision to be polished — not because the image needed to be.
>
> The test did not tell me that human work is better. It told me that I was mistaking polish for quality. That is a calibration error I would not have noticed without the blind test.

## Reverse-Direction Test

Take a rejected output.

Create a brief under which that output would actually be appropriate.

This prevents taste from becoming a rigid aesthetic dogma.

> [!example] Reverse-Direction Test in Practice
>
> **Rejected output:** A generated image that was too symmetrical, too clean, too "perfect."
>
> **New brief:** A portrait of a character who is trying to appear perfect while falling apart internally. The image should be symmetrical and clean on the surface, but the symmetry should feel oppressive.
>
> **Result:** The same image now works. The symmetry that seemed like a failure becomes a purposeful choice. The image is not different — the purpose is different.
>
> **What this teaches:** A rejected output is not universally bad. It is bad for the original purpose. The reverse-direction test forces you to articulate what purpose the output *could* serve, which in turn clarifies what purpose it *couldn't* serve.

## Prediction Test

Before generating, state which output type you expect to be strongest and why. State your confidence.

Compare prediction against outcome.

## Taste-Range Audit

> [!important] Calibration and Range Must Be Tracked Together
> See [[#Module 9 — Prediction & Calibration|Module 9, The taste-narrowness problem]].
>
> Every Part IV, ask: is my taste improving, or is it narrowing? The two can look the same on an anchor library. The distinguishing question is whether you are still encountering work that surprises you.

> [!example] Taste-Range Audit Questions
>
> - When did I last add an anchor that surprised me?
> - When did I last change my mind about something I thought I loved?
> - When did I last make something I did not know how to evaluate?
> - Is my anchor library becoming more diverse or more homogenous?
> - Am I demoting anchors because my criteria changed, or because I stopped thinking about them?

## Transfer Test

A new project is introduced in a medium or genre you did not use in the earlier anchor library.

Construct a provisional reference class and criteria before generating.

# Module 12 — Production Direction

[[#Table of Contents|↑ Back to TOC]]

## Goal

Run a project from problem to delivery while responding to external constraints without surrendering authorship.

## Project Loop

> [!important] Project Loop
> **Problem → Premise → Brief → Pipeline → First set → Diagnosis → Revision → External critique → Re-brief if necessary → Final → Defense**

## Key Distinction: Revision vs Re-Brief

Revise the output when the brief is correct and execution is failing.

Re-brief when the brief itself is wrong, incomplete, or no longer appropriate.

> [!warning] A Weak Director Patches a Broken Premise Indefinitely.
> A strong director can say: **"The work is pursuing the wrong problem."**

> [!example] Revision vs Re-Brief in Practice
>
> **Situation:** A client asks for a brand identity for a new coffee shop. The brief says "warm, inviting, artisanal." You produce a logo and visual system. The client says it feels "too corporate."
>
> **Revision response:** You soften the edges, warm the palette, add hand-drawn elements. You are patching the execution within the existing brief.
>
> **Re-brief response:** You ask the client what "corporate" means to them. They say it feels like it could be any coffee shop. You realize the brief was incomplete — "warm, inviting, artisanal" describes every coffee shop. The actual problem is differentiation. The coffee shop is in a neighborhood with six other coffee shops, all of which are warm, inviting, and artisanal.
>
> **The re-brief:** The brief is not "make it warm." The brief is "make it clearly *this* coffee shop and not the others." That changes everything — the logo, the palette, the typography, the voice. The old brief was not wrong; it was insufficient. The new brief is a different problem.
>
> **What a weak director does:** Keeps softening the logo, wondering why the client is still unhappy.
>
> **What a strong director does:** Says, "I think the problem is not the execution. I think we're solving the wrong problem. Can we re-brief?"

## Stakeholder Feedback Protocol

Never respond to feedback only at the sentence level.

First diagnose the feedback:

- What outcome is the stakeholder trying to change?
- Is the request about intent, audience, structure, execution, or personal preference?
- Does the requested change solve the underlying issue?
- What trade-off does it introduce?

Then choose:

- apply
- adapt
- propose alternative
- push back
- re-brief

> [!example] Diagnosing Feedback
>
> **Feedback:** "Can you make the logo bigger?"
>
> **Surface request:** Increase the size of the logo.
>
> **Underlying outcome the stakeholder wants:** The brand to be more visible and memorable.
>
> **Is the request about intent, audience, structure, execution, or preference?** Execution — but it may be a symptom of a structural problem.
>
> **Does the requested change solve the underlying issue?** Possibly not. A bigger logo is more visible, but visibility is not the same as memorability. If the logo is not memorable at its current size, making it bigger will just make a forgettable logo more noticeable.
>
> **What trade-off does it introduce?** A bigger logo may crowd the layout, compete with the product name, or feel desperate.
>
> **Possible responses:**
> - **Apply:** Make the logo bigger. (If the stakeholder is right and the logo is genuinely too small.)
> - **Adapt:** Increase the logo's contrast or placement rather than its size. (If visibility is the issue but size is not the solution.)
> - **Propose alternative:** Keep the logo size but increase its distinctiveness. (If memorability is the issue.)
> - **Push back:** Explain why a bigger logo will not solve the problem, and propose what will. (If you have a strong case.)
> - **Re-brief:** Ask what "visible and memorable" actually means for this brand. (If the underlying goal is unclear.)

## Revision Discipline

Default maximum: three major revision cycles.

Each cycle must have a specific objective.

The point is not to fetishise the number three. It is to prevent endless undirected patching.

> [!tip] Each Revision Cycle Needs a Name
> "Revision 1: Fix the focal hierarchy." "Revision 2: Strengthen the voice." "Revision 3: Resolve the ending." If you cannot name what the cycle is for, it is not a revision cycle — it is anxiety with a deadline.

# Module 14 — Collaborative Direction

[[#Table of Contents|↑ Back to TOC]]

## Goal

Learn to share authorship with an equal — not a client, not a critic, not a mentee — without defaulting to deference or dominance.

> [!important] A Contextual Competency
> Collaboration is a **Contextual competency**, not a universal requirement. It is required when your practice involves working with peers as co-creators. When it applies, it is not optional.
>
> See [[#What This Course Actually Trains|What This Course Actually Trains]].

## Core Distinctions

| Relationship | Decision structure |
|---|---|
| Direction (Module 12) | You decide; stakeholder influences |
| Critique (Appendix J) | You decide; critic informs |
| Mentorship (Module 19) | You scaffold; learner decides |
| **Collaboration** | **Decisions are shared; authorship is joint** |

## What Collaboration Requires

- **Explicit authorship agreement** — who is credited for what, and how
- **Decision rights map** — which decisions are yours, theirs, and joint
- **Taste conflict protocol** — how disagreements are resolved when both positions are legitimate
- **Economics protocol** — how revenue, credit, and cost are split
- **Exit protocol** — what happens if the collaboration ends
- **Credit protocol** — how the work is attributed publicly

## Decision Rights Map

For any collaborative project, produce a table:

| Decision type | Owner | Consultation | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Premise | Joint | Both | Neither can veto |
| Visual direction | A | B | B has final veto on ethical concerns |
| Writing voice | B | A | A has final veto on factual accuracy |
| Pipeline | Joint | Both | Each owns their domain |
| Revisions | Joint | Both | Requires agreement |
| Delivery format | A | B | A owns client relationship |
| Credit | Joint | Both | Written agreement before starting |

> [!example] Why Decision Rights Matter
>
> Two collaborators — a visual artist and a writer — are working on an illustrated book. They agree on the premise and the structure. But when it comes to the visual style, they disagree. The artist wants a loose, expressive style. The writer wants something more controlled and precise, to match the tone of the prose.
>
> Without a decision rights map, this disagreement could stall the project or damage the relationship. With a map, they have already agreed that visual direction belongs to the artist, with the writer having consultation rights but not veto — except on ethical concerns. The writer can state their preference, but the artist decides. The writer's contribution is respected, but the decision is clear.
>
> The map does not eliminate disagreement. It gives the disagreement a structure. The writer can say, "I disagree, but I understand this is your domain." The artist can say, "I hear you, but I'm making this call." The relationship survives because the roles were defined before the conflict.

## Taste Conflict Protocol

When both positions are legitimate and neither is obviously wrong:

1. **State the disagreement at the correct level.** Is this about premise, structure, execution, or preference?
2. **Name the mechanism behind each position.** What does each position accomplish that the other does not?
3. **Identify the purpose.** Which position better serves the stated intention?
4. **Test if possible.** Can either position be tested against an audience, a reference, or a small experiment?
5. **Decide.** If no test is possible, decide by:
   - domain ownership (the person who owns that domain decides)
   - alternation (each person's position is used in a variation)
   - escalation (a third party is consulted)
   - concession (one person concedes, and the record states why)
6. **Record the decision and the reasoning.** The record matters even when the outcome is temporary.

## Resolving the "Both Positions Are Right" Problem

> [!important] Some Conflicts Are Not Resolvable by Argument
> When two competent directors disagree on a legitimate aesthetic choice, the disagreement is often not about correctness — it is about **which of two valid things the work will be**. The resolution is not to find the right answer. The resolution is to choose, record the choice, and live with its consequences as a shared work.

> [!example] Both Positions Are Right
>
> A photographer and a writer are collaborating on a photo essay. The photographer wants a sequence that builds slowly, with a quiet opening and a gradual intensification. The writer wants a sequence that opens with the strongest image and then complicates it.
>
> Both sequences work. The slow build creates a sense of immersion. The strong opening creates a sense of urgency. Neither is wrong. The disagreement is about which of two valid experiences the work will be.
>
> The resolution is not to find the "correct" sequence. The resolution is to choose one, commit to it, and let the other go. The choice may be made by domain ownership (the photographer owns sequencing), by alternation (each version is tested on a small audience), or by concession (one person says, "I trust you on this one").
>
> The important thing is that the choice is recorded. Six months later, when someone asks why the sequence opens the way it does, the collaborators can say: "We debated this. We chose this. Here's why."

## The Economics of Collaboration

> [!important] A Collaboration Can Survive a Taste Conflict and Still Dissolve Over an Unspoken Assumption About Who Gets Paid What
> Taste conflict is visible. Economics is invisible. If economics is not agreed before starting, it becomes a silent fracture.

## What Must Be Agreed Before Starting

| Question | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| How is revenue split? | Equal? By contribution? By role? By seniority? |
| How is credit ordered? | Bylines, cover credit, exhibition labels |
| How are costs shared? | Tools, materials, travel, studio, fees |
| What counts as a contribution? | Time? Capital? Ideas? Craft? Network? |
| What if contributions are unequal? | Does the split track time, value, or ownership? |
| Who owns the pipeline and process knowledge? | The system may be more valuable than the output |
| What happens on dissolution? | Who keeps what? Who can reuse what? |
| What if one person wants to stop? | Does the other have the right to continue? |
| Who has final authority on financial decisions? | Fees, discounts, refunds |
| How are disputes resolved? | Mediation? Third party? Split? |

## Revenue Models

| Model | Use when |
|---|---|
| Equal split | Contributions are genuinely equal |
| Weighted split | Contributions are unequal in an agreed way |
| Role-based split | Different roles (lead / support) are pre-defined |
| Milestone-based | Payments tied to defined project stages |
| Post-sale royalty | Both parties benefit from long-tail value |
| Fixed fee + royalty | One party is compensated upfront, the other benefits from upside |

## Credit-Linked Payment

Some collaborations link payment to credit order — e.g., first-named author receives a higher share. This is legitimate if agreed in advance. It is not legitimate if imposed after the fact.

> [!warning] Write It Before You Need It
> Economic agreements are easy to make at the start and impossible to make cleanly at the end. The agreement is not a sign of distrust. It is a sign that the collaboration matters enough to protect.

## Procedure — Collaboration Drill

Work with one collaborator on a small project (2–4 weeks).

Before starting:

1. Write the authorship agreement.
2. Complete the decision rights map.
3. Agree on the taste conflict protocol.
4. Agree on the **economics protocol**.
5. Agree on the exit protocol.

During the project:

- Record every joint decision in the decision log.
- Record every disagreement in a separate conflict log.
- Note which decisions were easy, which were hard, and which remain unresolved.

After the project:

- Reconstruct the collaboration.
- State what worked, what did not, and what you would change.

## Conflict Log Template

| Field | Content |
|---|---|
| Disagreement | What we disagreed about |
| Positions | What each of us held |
| Level | Premise / structure / execution / preference |
| Mechanism | What each position accomplished |
| Resolution | How it was resolved |
| Loss | What was lost by the chosen outcome |
| Economic dimension | Was money, credit, or ownership at stake? |

## Transfer Test

Given a hypothetical taste conflict with a co-director, produce a written resolution that:

- states both positions accurately
- names the underlying mechanism of disagreement
- proposes a resolution
- records what is lost by each possible outcome

## Transfer Test (Economics)

Given a hypothetical collaboration with unequal contributions, propose:

- a revenue split with reasoning
- a credit order with reasoning

> **What you refuse:** Using a statistic you cannot verify.
>
> **Why:** The refusal is not about the client's goal. It is about the difference between persuasion and manipulation. An unverified statistic may persuade, but it does so by compromising the truth.
>
> **What you offer instead:** "I can't use that number without a source. But I can help you find a verified statistic that makes the same point, or I can strengthen the argument using evidence we already have."
>
> **What you record:** The request, the principle, the refusal, the alternative, and the result.

# Module 15 — Authorship as Position

[[#Table of Contents|↑ Back to TOC]]

## Goal

Move from "I can make things" to "I stand for something, and the work demonstrates it."

## Authorship Is a Pattern of Commitments

A position should answer:

1. What am I trying to understand or change?
2. What kind of audience do I care about?
3. What do I repeatedly notice that others may overlook?
4. What formal or rhetorical mechanisms do I trust?
5. What will I not do merely because it is effective?
6. What kinds of work do I want to become better at making?
7. What problems do I keep choosing to pursue, even when nobody asks?

> [!example] What a Position Looks Like
>
> **What I am trying to understand:** How people perform competence while feeling like frauds. The gap between the surface of expertise and the experience of doubt.
>
> **What audience I care about:** People who make things and worry they are not real makers. Not beginners — people who have been doing it long enough to know the doubt does not go away.
>
> **What I repeatedly notice:** The small adjustments people make when they think no one is watching. The moment before a performance begins. The pause after a question is asked.
>
> **What mechanisms I trust:** Close observation. The specific detail that carries more weight than the general claim. Silence. What is not said.
>
> **What I will not do:** Pretend certainty I do not have. Use complexity to hide emptiness. Make work that flatters the audience instead of challenging them.
>
> **What I want to get better at:** Writing that moves without manipulating. Images that suggest without explaining. Structures that hold complexity without collapsing into chaos.
>
> **What problems I keep choosing:** The gap between how things appear and how they feel. The performance of confidence. The invisible labour of maintaining a surface.

## Private Position

A private position is for you.

It can be uncertain, contradictory, exploratory, and unfinished.

It does not need to be coherent. It needs to be honest.

## Public Position

A public position states what you can responsibly claim and demonstrate through work.

It is not a manifesto. It is a statement of what you stand for, backed by evidence.

> [!warning] The Gap Between Private and Public Is Not Hypocrisy
> The private position is what you are working through. The public position is what you can responsibly claim. The gap between them is the space of your practice. It is not a failure. It is the sign that you are still learning.

## Position Test

Look at 10 finished pieces without titles or explanations.

Ask:

> What commitments can another person infer from the work itself?

Then compare that inferred position against the written position.

> [!tip] The Gap Is Useful Evidence.
> If the inferred position is different from the written one, the work is doing something you have not yet articulated. This is not a problem. It is information. The gap tells you what you are actually making, as opposed to what you think you are making.

> [!example] What the Position Test Reveals
>
> I wrote that my position was about "the gap between performance and doubt." But when I looked at 10 pieces without titles, the inferred position was different. The work was about "the moment of transition — the second before something begins or after it ends." The doubt was there, but it was not the subject. The subject was the threshold.
>
> The gap was useful. It told me that my written position was aspirational — what I wanted to be making. The work was actually about something else. I revised the written position to match what the work was doing. The revision was more honest, and more useful.

|---|---|
| What I am trying to understand or change | |
| Who I make work for | |
| What I repeatedly notice | |
| What mechanisms I trust | |
| What I refuse to do | |
| What I want to get better at | |
| What problems I keep choosing | |
| What the work actually demonstrates | |

# Module 16 — Self-Directed Practice

[[#Table of Contents|↑ Back to TOC]]

## Goal

Deliberately remove course scaffolding.

## You Receive Only

- a time limit
- available resources
- audience or situation
- medium restrictions, if any
- external practical constraints

You do **not** receive:

- a problem statement
- a premise
- a brief
- acceptance criteria
- references
- a required tool
- a specified output format

You must construct those.

## Self-Directed Protocol

1. Observe the situation.
2. Identify several possible problems.
3. Choose one problem worth pursuing and state why.
4. Generate three premises.
5. Select one.
6. Write the brief.
7. Choose the medium and process.
8. Build and test.
9. Curate.
10. Seek critique.
11. Revise or re-brief.
12. Defend the result.
13. State what the next iteration should investigate.

## Important Condition

You must be able to explain **why the chosen problem was worth pursuing**.

This is different from explaining why the final artifact looks the way it does.

> [!example] What Self-Directed Practice Looks Like
>
> **Situation:** I am in a city I have never visited. I have three days. I have a camera and a notebook. I have no assignment.
>
> **Observation:** I notice that the city has a lot of public stairways. They are not just functional — they are social spaces. People sit on them, meet on them, perform on them.
>
> **Possible problems:**
> 1. The stairway as a social architecture.
> 2. The difference between how a city is designed and how it is used.
> 3. The way visitors notice things residents ignore.
>
> **Selected problem:** The stairway as a social architecture. Why this one? Because it is specific, it is observable, and it has tension — the stairs were designed for movement, but they are being used for gathering.
>
> **Premise:** A stairway is a space that is designed to move through but often becomes a space to stay.
>
> **Brief:** Photograph five stairways. In each, there is at least one person who is not moving. The image should feel like a portrait of a place, not a photograph of a person. The person should be small in the frame. The stairway should be the subject.
>
> **What I made:** A series of five photographs. They are not masterpieces. But they are mine. I found the problem, wrote the brief, made the work, and can defend it.

## The Unassigned Beginning

> [!important] Once a month, after the intensive
> Start with no brief, no target deliverable, and no predetermined medium. Spend 60 minutes finding something worth pursuing. Then stop.

Why stop?

Because otherwise you can become very disciplined about **doing projects** while slowly losing the ability to **notice projects**.

The practice is the act of beginning without external instruction.

If you always continue into production, you are training project execution. If you sometimes stop after the beginning, you are training the faculty that makes projects possible.

## Record

| Field | Content |
|---|---|
| Date | |
| What I began | |
| How I found it | |
| Why it held my attention | |
| Did I continue? | Yes / No |
| If no, why not | |

Store entries in a dedicated section of the **Problem-Finding Log**.

# Module 17 — Cross-Medium Synthesis

[[#Table of Contents|↑ Back to TOC]]

## Goal

Fuse visual and writing into one authored object rather than alternating between them.

> [!important] The Untested Thesis
> The course claims a "shared architecture of judgment" across visual and writing. That claim is stated but not tested by alternation alone. Integration tests whether the shared architecture is real.

## What Counts as Synthesis

Not:
- an image with a caption
- a text with an illustration
- an alternation between visual and verbal sections

But:
- a book where image sequencing and text both carry the argument
- a zine where the visual composition and the prose argue together
- an installation where wall text is a compositional element, not a label
- a sequence where sound, image, and language are co-equal

## Required Translation

Move from:

> premise → what the image must do → what the text must do → what neither can do alone → how they argue together

## Procedure — Synthesis Drill

1. Select a premise from an earlier part.
2. Decide what the visual mode is responsible for.
3. Decide what the writing mode is responsible for.
4. Identify the third thing — what neither mode can do alone.
5. Produce a small synthesis work (spread, sequence, page, installation).
6. Diagnose: does the synthesis work as one object, or as two objects next to each other?
7. Revise or re-brief.

## Test of Synthesis

The synthesis succeeds if removing either mode would leave the work unable to make its argument.

If the image can be removed and the text still works, they are not in synthesis. They are illustrated.

If the text can be removed and the image still works, they are not in synthesis. They are captioned.

> [!example] What Synthesis Looks Like
>
> **Premise:** A person can be surrounded by evidence of belonging while experiencing increasing evidence of irrelevance.
>
> **What the image must do:** Show the evidence of belonging — the photographs, the objects, the room — without showing the person's face. The image carries the context.
>
> **What the text must do:** Carry the interior voice. The text does not describe the room. It describes the experience of being in the room.
>
> **What neither can do alone:** The gap between the room and the experience. The image shows the room. The text shows the experience. The synthesis shows the gap.
>
> **The form:** A spread. The left page is a photograph of a domestic interior — warm, cluttered, full of life. The right page is a single paragraph, set in a quiet typeface, describing the experience of standing in that room and feeling nothing.
>
> **The test:** If you remove the photograph, the text becomes abstract. If you remove the text, the photograph becomes sentimental. Neither works alone. The synthesis is the gap.

## Transfer Test

You are given a premise and a limitation: neither the visual alone nor the writing alone can carry it.

Construct a synthesis solution from first principles.

3. **positionality check for each candidate problem**

5. three candidate premises
6. selected premise and rejection logic
7. self-authored brief
8. chosen production process

10. diagnosis
11. revision or re-brief
12. final work
13. defense
14. transfer reflection

## The Transfer Reflection Asks

> What principle from the course did I use without being told to use it?

> What did I ignore from the course, and why?

> What did I discover that the course had not taught me?

> What problem did I find that nobody gave me?

> What part of the process now feels like my own practice rather than an exercise?

## The Decisive Question

> [!question] **If the course disappeared tomorrow, would I still know how to begin?**

A convincing answer requires evidence, not optimism.

> [!example] What a Blank-Sheet Test Looks Like
>
> **Situation:** I am given a room, a camera, a notebook, and four hours. No other instructions.
>
> **Observation:** The room is a waiting area. It has chairs, a window, a plant, a clock, and a stack of magazines from 2019. The room is designed for waiting. But nobody is waiting.
>
> **Candidate problems:**
> 1. The waiting room as a space of suspended time.
> 2. The difference between a space designed for an activity and a space where that activity does not occur.
> 3. The way a room ages — the magazines from 2019, the plant that has been watered, the clock that still works.
>
> **Positionality check:**
> 4. I am not waiting. I am observing. I am outside the experience.
> 5. I am adjacent. I have waited in rooms like this, but not this room.
> 6. I am inside the experience of noticing. The room is old. So am I.
>
> **Selected problem:** The waiting room as a space of suspended time. I have standing because I have experienced waiting. I am not speaking for anyone. I am speaking from my own experience of a universal condition.
>
> **Premise:** A waiting room is a space where time is supposed to pass but does not.
>
> **Brief:** Photograph the room without any people. The absence is the point. The room should feel like it is holding its breath.
>
> **What I made:** Five photographs. The clock is in all of them. The magazines are in three. The empty chairs are in all of them.
>
> **Defense:** The absence of people is the subject. The room is designed for waiting, but nobody is waiting. The emptiness is not a failure of the room. It is the room's true condition.
>
> **What I learned:** I found this problem. Nobody gave it to me. I did not know I was going to find it. I began with nothing and ended with something I can defend.
>
> **If the course disappeared tomorrow:** I would still know how to begin. I would find a room. I would observe. I would find a problem. I would make something.

# Independent Practice (Months 4–9)

[[#Table of Contents|↑ Back to TOC]]

## The Purpose of Independent Practice

The intensive teaches you a language for judgment.

Independent practice tests whether that language has become behaviour.

The central variable is **scaffolding removal**.

Each month should require you to author more of the upstream decision-making.

> [!important] The Shift
> In the intensive, the course supplies the structure. In independent practice, you supply it. The course becomes a reference you consult, not a schedule you follow.

---

## The Monthly Progression

### Month 4 — Directed Execution

**Scaffold source:** An external brief — a real opportunity, a peer-issued challenge, or a self-written brief you commit to following without modification.

**Your responsibility:** execute, diagnose, revise, defend.

> [!example] What Month 4 Looks Like
>
> A friend asks you to design a poster for their band's show. They give you the date, the venue, the bands, and a vague sense of the vibe ("something that feels like late-night driving"). You write the brief yourself — premise, intention, constraints, acceptance criteria — and then execute it. The external brief is the constraint. Your job is to work within it.

### Month 5 — Brief Ownership

**Scaffold source:** A broad project category you select.

**Your responsibility:** define audience, premise, intention, constraints, references, and criteria.

> [!example] What Month 5 Looks Like
>
> You decide to make a series of short essays about your neighborhood. You choose the category. But you still need to define the premise, the audience, the constraints. The category is the only thing supplied. Everything else is yours.

### Month 6 — Premise Ownership

**Scaffold source:** A theme or broad constraint you select or receive externally.

**Your responsibility:** determine what is actually worth exploring.

> [!example] What Month 6 Looks Like
>
> You decide to work on "the experience of being new somewhere." That is the theme. But what does that mean? What is the actual premise? What is the tension? What is worth making? The theme is the starting point, not the destination.

### Month 7 — Problem Discovery

**Scaffold source:** A real or simulated stakeholder environment where the stated problem may not be the actual problem.

**Your responsibility:** diagnose the situation before accepting the brief.

> [!example] What Month 7 Looks Like
>
> A local business asks you to redesign their website. They say the problem is "it looks outdated." You visit the business. You talk to customers. You realize the actual problem is not the website — it is that customers do not understand what the business does. The website is a symptom. The problem is positioning. You re-brief the project before designing anything.

### Month 8 — Self-Originated Practice

**Scaffold source:** None.

**Your responsibility:** choose the subject, premise, and medium.

**External role:** calibration partner, not project manager.

> [!example] What Month 8 Looks Like
>
> You wake up. There is no brief, no theme, no category. You have to find something. You spend a week observing, reading, walking. You find a problem. You make a premise. You write a brief. You build the work. You critique it. You revise it. You defend it. You did all of it.

### Month 9 — Blank-Sheet Capstone

**Scaffold source:** None.

**Your responsibility:** originate the entire project from real-world conditions only — including the problem itself.

> [!important] Month 9 Is the Course's Final Test
> If you can do Month 9, you have internalised the course. If you cannot, you have identified what needs more practice. Both are useful outcomes.

---

## The Monthly Cycle

Each month follows a four-week rhythm, but the source of the decisions changes over time.

| Week | Activity |
|---|---|
| **1** | Problem discovery + premise + brief |
| **2** | Build + generate + diagnose |
| **3** | External critique + re-brief decision |
| **4** | Revision + delivery + reflection + transfer test |

Every month includes:

- at least one rejected alternative
- decision log
- external critique
- one explicit uncertainty
- one transfer reflection
- practice log entries
- **one problem-finding session** (see below)
- **one unassigned beginning** (see below)
- **one protected non-instrumental session**
- **one play session**
- **calibration log entries**

> [!tip] Keep the Monthly Cycle Light
> The monthly cycle is a rhythm, not a rigid structure. If you spend the whole month on one project, that is fine. If you spend it on three small ones, that is also fine. The point is that each month involves finding a problem, making something, getting feedback, revising, and reflecting. The exact allocation of weeks is less important than the presence of the cycle.

# The Monthly Problem-Finding Session

[[#Table of Contents|↑ Back to TOC]]

> [!important] The Maintenance System Must Exercise the Upstream Faculty, Not Only the Downstream One
> A practitioner can become extremely good at executing independently supplied ideas while believing they have achieved independence. The monthly problem-finding session is the antidote.

## Purpose

Keep exercising the faculty that sits **before** the brief.

Without this practice, problem-finding atrophies even as production and curation improve.

## Protocol

Once a month, before choosing a project:

**Spend 60–90 minutes identifying something worth pursuing without an assigned brief.**

You may use any source:

- a walk
- a book
- a document
- a dataset
- a conversation
- a news event
- a space
- a personal concern
- an unresolved tension from your own work

## Record

For each session, record:

| Field | Content |
|---|---|
| Date | |
| Source | What I attended to |
| Observation | What I noticed |
| Question | What is unresolved |
| Tension | What cannot comfortably coexist |
| Mode | Solve / Investigate / Reveal / Reframe / Explore / Construct |
| Possible premise | A proposition worth exploring |
| Stakes | Why it matters, in what domain |
| Positionality | From / With / For / About |
| Would I pursue this? | Yes / No / Maybe |

## Output Requirement

The session must produce at least **three candidate problems** and a justification for the one (if any) you would pursue.

You do **not** have to produce the work.

The objective is to keep the upstream creative faculty alive.

## Log Integration

Store the session output in your **Problem-Finding Log** (see [[#Module 2 — Problem-Finding|Module 2]]).

Over 6 months of independent practice, this log becomes a longitudinal record of your independent creative attention.

## Why This Matters for the Ultimate Goal

> [!question] **When nobody asks you to make anything at all, can you find a problem worth pursuing?**
> The monthly problem-finding session is the rehearsal for that question. Without it, the course's final question remains aspirational rather than trained.

# The Unassigned Beginning

[[#Table of Contents|↑ Back to TOC]]

> [!important] An Unassigned Beginning Is Not the Same as a Problem-Finding Session
> A problem-finding session exercises the faculty. An unassigned beginning exercises the *decision to begin*.

## Purpose

Preserve the ability to **notice projects**, not only the ability to **do projects**.

A practitioner can become very disciplined about executing projects while slowly losing the capacity to originate them. The unassigned beginning is the recurring practice that prevents this.

## Protocol

Once a month:

**Start with no brief, no target deliverable, and no predetermined medium.**

Spend 60 minutes finding something worth pursuing.

Then stop.

You do not have to make the project.

You do not have to decide what it is.

You do not have to commit.

## Why Stopping Is the Point

The practice is not the project. The practice is the act of beginning without external instruction.

If you always continue into production, you are training project execution. If you sometimes stop after the beginning, you are training the faculty that makes projects possible.

## Record

| Field | Content |
|---|---|
| Date | |
| What I began | |
| How I found it | |
| Why it held my attention | |
| Did I continue? | Yes / No |
| If no, why not | |

Store entries in a dedicated section of the **Problem-Finding Log**.

## Relationship to Other Practices

| Practice | What it trains |
|---|---|
| Non-instrumental attention | The faculty of noticing |
| Problem-finding session | The faculty of locating problems |
| Unassigned beginning | The faculty of starting without instruction |
| Project work | The faculty of execution |

All four are necessary. None substitutes for another.

# Protected Non-Instrumental Attention

[[#Table of Contents|↑ Back to TOC]]

> [!important] A Counter-Practice to the Course's Own Rigour
> The problem-finding sequence (Notice → Collect → Frame → Test → Select → Justify) is powerful. But if it becomes the only mode of attention, the practitioner loses access to material that cannot be searched for.

## Purpose

Protect the space where serendipity operates.

## Protocol

Once a week, spend 30–60 minutes in one of the following:

- a walk without a destination
- reading without an objective
- looking at images without evaluating them
- sitting in a public space
- listening without recording
- handling materials without making anything
- listening to sound without analysing it

## Rules

During the session:

- do not take notes aimed at a project
- do not evaluate anything as good or bad
- do not decide whether anything is useful
- do not convert any of it into a problem or premise

After the session:

- do nothing with it

## Why This Is Not Wasted Time

Serendipity is an input to the problem-finding process that cannot be generated by the problem-finding process itself. Without protected non-instrumental attention, the practitioner's material slowly narrows to what they already know how to look for.

> [!important] The Material That Surfaces from Non-Instrumental Attention Is Often the Material That Makes the Work Distinctive.
> If every perception is immediately converted into productivity, that material never surfaces.

## Log

After the session, write one line:

> What did I encounter that I was not looking for?

Do not analyse it. Do not integrate it.

Over time, these entries become a record of what your attention does when it is not being directed.

# Module 19 — Teaching the Framework

[[#Table of Contents|↑ Back to TOC]]

## Goal

Learn to scaffold someone else's problem-finding, premise generation, and diagnostic vision — without doing the work for them.

> [!important] A Contextual Competency
> Transmission is a **Contextual competency**, not a universal requirement.
>
> Teaching is valuable evidence of internalisation, but it is **not necessary for creative agency**. A person can be an exceptional artist, writer, filmmaker, or independent researcher without teaching anyone.
>
> **Transmission is a high-order test of whether the framework has become portable knowledge. It is not a prerequisite for authorship.**
>
> It is required when your practice involves mentoring, teaching, leading a team, or collaborating with less experienced practitioners.

## The Core Skill

> [!important] Scaffolding Is Not Solving
> Teaching the framework means **removing the scaffolding in the right order for someone else.** Not telling them what to do. Not doing it for them. Not handing them your answer.

## The Transmission Ladder

Just as your own course removed scaffolding in sequence, you remove scaffolding for another person in sequence:

| Stage | You supply | You remove |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | The problem and the brief | — |
| 2 | The problem | The brief |
| 3 | The category | The problem |
| 4 | The situation | The category |
| 5 | The conditions | The situation |
| 6 | Nothing | Everything except the question "What's worth doing here?" |

At each stage, the learner does more of the upstream work. You do less.

## Diagnostic Questions to Ask Instead of Giving Answers

| Instead of… | Ask… |
|---|---|
| "You should make it about X." | "What's the tension you keep returning to?" |
| "The composition doesn't work." | "What is the image trying to make happen, and where does the eye actually go?" |
| "Try this prompt." | "What variable are you trying to control?" |
| "This is generic." | "Is this a decision or a default?" |
| "You need a stronger premise." | "What do you currently believe about this?" |
| "Revise it." | "What's the highest unresolved level, and what would you test first?" |
| "That's wrong." | "What evidence would change your mind?" |

## Diagnosing the Learner's Level

Before scaffolding, diagnose where the learner is stuck:

| Symptom | Likely level | Response |
|---|---|---|
| Cannot find a problem | Problem-finding | Model a problem-finding session |
| Finds problems but not premises | Premise | Ask them to state what they believe |
| Premises but vague specs | Specification | Require acceptance criteria |
| Specs but weak diagnosis | Diagnosis | Ask what level the problem sits at |
| Diagnosis but no curation criteria | Curation | Require reference class and mechanism |
| Criteria but unstable process | Systems | Require variable map and prediction |
| Systems but no direction | Direction | Introduce real constraints |
| Direction but no position | Authorship | Ask what they repeatedly choose |
| Position but no transfer | Independence | Remove a scaffold and observe |

## What You Must Not Do

- Do not supply the premise
- Do not solve the diagnostic problem for them
- Do not tell them what their work is about
- Do not rescue them from productive uncertainty
- Do not confuse your taste with their direction
- Do not make their decisions and call it mentorship

## The Hard Test

> [!question] The Transmission Test
> **Can you scaffold someone else's problem-finding without supplying the problem?**

If you cannot, you have not yet internalised the framework at the level where you can hand it on.

## Feedback on Your Teaching

> [!important] You Can Complete the Transmission Drill, Believe You Withheld the Answer Correctly, and Still Have Taught Badly
> Teaching is a practice with its own feedback loop. Your read of "what they discovered that I wouldn't have" is not sufficient evidence that your scaffolding worked.

## The Three-Source Feedback Loop

Teaching feedback comes from three sources:

| Source | What it tells you | How to get it |
|---|---|---|
| **The learner** | Whether your questions opened a next step or closed one | Ask the learner directly — see questions below |
| **A third-party observer** | Whether you supplied the answer without realizing it | Invite someone to watch a session and describe what they saw |
| **Your own record** | Whether the pattern of your scaffolding is consistent | Record sessions and review them with delay |

## Questions to Ask the Learner After Scaffolding

Do not ask "Did that help?" That is a polite question and produces a polite answer.

Ask instead:

> What did you decide after we talked?

> What did you still not know?

> Did any of my questions tell you what to do?

> Was there a moment you wanted me to just give you the answer? What happened next?

> What would you have done if I had not been there?

## Third-Party Observation Protocol

At least once during Module 19, ask a third party to observe a scaffolding session.

Give them this instruction:

> Watch the session. Note the moments where I supplied an answer instead of opening a question. Note the moments where I let the learner sit in uncertainty. Note what happened after each.

After the session, ask the observer:

> Where did I do the work for them?

> Where did I withhold appropriately?

> What did the learner discover that they would not have discovered alone?

> What did the learner miss because of something I did or did not say?

## Self-Recording Review

Record a scaffolding session. Wait one week. Watch it.

Note:

- where you spoke first
- where you answered a question with a question
- where you named the answer
- where you tolerated silence
- where you filled silence with your own thinking

> [!warning] The Most Common Teaching Failure
> Filling silence. The learner pauses because they are thinking. The teacher pauses because they are uncomfortable. The teacher speaks. The learner accepts the teacher's answer. The session appears successful. The learner has learned nothing.

## Procedure — Transmission Drill

1. Select one person you can work with (peer, junior, student, collaborator).
2. Diagnose their current level using the table above.
3. Scaffold at the appropriate stage — no more, no less.
4. Record what you supplied, what you removed, and what they produced.
5. Note where you were tempted to do the work for them.
6. Note what they discovered that you would not have discovered yourself.
7. **Get feedback on your teaching from the learner and (if possible) a third-party observer.**
8. **Compare your own read of the session against the feedback.**

## Teaching Feedback Record

| Field | Entry |
|---|---|
| Session | |
| Learner | |
| What I supplied | |
| What I removed | |
| Where I was tempted to do the work | |
| Learner's account | What they decided, what they still don't know |
| Observer's account | Where I did the work for them |
| My read vs their read | Gap between what I thought and what happened |
| Revision | What I'll change next time |

## Transfer Test

Given a person stuck at a specific point in the creative loop, produce:

- a diagnosis of their actual bottleneck
- a scaffold that does not supply the answer
- a question you would ask instead of giving direction

## Transfer Test (Teaching Feedback)

Take a recording of a scaffolding session.

From the recording alone, identify:

- where you supplied an answer
- where you opened a question
- where you filled silence
- what the learner actually decided

Compare your reading against the learner's memory of the session.

# The Decision Log

[[#Table of Contents|↑ Back to TOC]]

The decision log records not everything that happened, but every decision whose reversal would materially change the work.

## Format

| Field | Required content |
|---|---|
| Context | What was the problem? |
| Decision | What did I choose? |
| Alternative | What did I reject? |
| Reason | Why did I choose this? |
| Evidence | What supported the decision? |
| Risk | What might make it wrong? |
| Result | What happened? |
| Revision | What changed in my understanding? |

## The Log Must Record Uncertainty

> [!important] A Sophisticated Decision Is Not One That Pretends Certainty.
> You should be able to write: "I chose A over B because..., but I was uncertain about X. The test showed Y, which changed my view of Z."

This is more useful than a retrospective story in which every decision appears inevitable.

> [!example] What a Decision Log Entry Looks Like
>
> **Context:** The essay's opening was too slow. The first 500 words were context-setting before any tension appeared.
>
> **Decision:** Cut the first 500 words entirely and open with the scene that currently appears on page 3.
>
> **Alternative:** Keep the opening but compress it to 200 words.
>
> **Reason:** The compressed version still delays the tension. The reader has to wait. Opening with the scene gives the reader a reason to keep going.
>
> **Evidence:** I tested both versions on two readers. Both said the second version made them want to keep reading. Neither said the first version was bad — they said it was slow.
>
> **Risk:** The cut opening might lose necessary context. The reader might not know where they are or who is speaking.
>
> **Result:** The context was not lost. It was implied by the scene. The reader understood more from the scene than from the explanation.
>
> **Revision:** I now understand that context can be carried by action, not only by exposition. I had been explaining because I did not trust the scene to do the work.

## For Collaborative Projects

In collaborative work, the decision log records joint decisions.

A separate **conflict log** records disagreements that were resolved, and how.

| Field | Content |
|---|---|
| Disagreement | What we disagreed about |
| Positions | What each of us held |
| Level | Premise / structure / execution / preference |
| Mechanism | What each position accomplished |
| Resolution | How it was resolved |
| Loss | What was lost by the chosen outcome |
| Economic dimension | Was money, credit, or ownership at stake? |

# Calibration Network Protocol

[[#Table of Contents|↑ Back to TOC]]

> [!important] No Instructor Means You Must Build Your Own Feedback Infrastructure.
> This is not optional. Isolation produces blind spots.

## Not Just Critics

The people who help you calibrate are not merely critics. Some represent audience, some represent craft, some represent domain knowledge, some represent ordinary perception. Build the network deliberately.

## Building a Calibration Network

Identify 2–5 people who will give honest feedback across different roles.

| Role | What they calibrate |
|---|---|
| Audience | Whether the work reaches the intended reader or viewer |
| Craft expert | Whether the execution holds at the level of the medium |
| Domain knowledge | Whether the content is accurate or appropriate |
| Ordinary perception | Whether the work is legible without context |
| Peer / co-creator | Whether the decisions are defensible |

## Do Not Ask

> "Is this good?"

## Ask Instead

> What did you think this was trying to do?

> Where did you feel most engaged?

> Where did your attention or understanding change?

> What seemed unnecessary?

> What did you expect that the work did not provide?

> What felt generic or familiar?

> What decision appears strongest?

> What decision appears weakest?

> What would you change first if you owned the project?

## After Critique

Diagnose the feedback before deciding whether to use it.

> [!warning] Feedback Is Not Authority Merely Because It Is External.

Record:

- feedback received
- underlying problem inferred
- whether it maps to the brief
- whether it reveals a blind spot
- whether to apply, adapt, reject, or use it to re-brief

## Critique Record Template

| Field | Content |
|---|---|
| Source | Who said it |
| Role | Audience / craft / domain / ordinary perception / peer |
| Feedback | What they said |
| Inferred problem | What underlying issue it points to |
| Maps to brief? | Yes / No / Partially |
| Blind spot? | What I could not see |
| Decision | Apply / Adapt / Reject / Re-brief |
| Reasoning | Why |
| Result | What happened |

## When You Cannot Get External Critique

Use the structured self-critique protocol in [[#Appendix J — Feedback, Critique & Self-Calibration|Appendix J]].

# Failure Protocols

[[#Table of Contents|↑ Back to TOC]]

The course distinguishes **four kinds of failure**. Each has its own protocol.

> [!important] Different Failures Need Different Responses
> Most courses have one failure protocol. But output failure, habit failure, motivational failure, and log decay are structurally different problems. Treating them identically produces the wrong intervention.

## Failure Type 1 — Output Failure

**What it is:** The work does not achieve its stated intention.

**Diagnosis:** This is a craft, decision, or execution problem.

### Output Failure Protocol

1. State the intended outcome.
2. State what actually happened.
3. Identify the highest-level failure.
4. Identify which decision created it.
5. Determine whether the problem was:
   - problem-finding
   - positionality
   - observation
   - premise
   - specification
   - system
   - curation
   - direction
   - craft knowledge
   - calibration
6. Choose the smallest meaningful experiment.
7. Re-run.
8. Record what changed.

### Extension Conditions

Extend the month when:

- you never identified the real problem
- you patched the artifact without re-briefing
- you cannot explain the acceptance criteria
- the pipeline was copied without understanding
- external critique was accepted or rejected without diagnosis
- you produced polished output but could not reconstruct the decision path

---

## Failure Type 2 — Habit Failure

**What it is:** You did not do the practice. Not once, but as a pattern.

**Diagnosis:** This is not a work problem. It is an environment, structure, or energy problem. Treating it as a work problem ("I need to try harder") rarely fixes it.

### Common Patterns

| Pattern | Underlying cause | Correction |
|---|---|---|
| Skipping practice sessions | Too vague; no fixed time | Schedule specific blocks; prepare materials the night before |
| Skipping non-instrumental attention | Feels unproductive | Re-read Module 3; the session is the practice |
| Skipping problem-finding | Feels like it delays work | Re-read Module 2; the practice is the work |
| Skipping logs | Feels like admin | Simplify the log; one line is enough |
| Skipping critique | Avoidance of judgment | Name what you fear; get one small piece of feedback |
| Repeatedly extending deadlines | Perfectionism or scope creep | Cut scope; deliver something |
| Working but not recording | Belief that the work speaks for itself | Record one decision per session |
| Avoiding a specific part | Hidden difficulty | Diagnose which competency is actually blocked |

### Habit Failure Protocol

1. **Name the pattern.** Not "I missed a session" but "I have skipped three weeks of practice."
2. **Identify the cause.** Environment? Energy? Fear? Boredom? Vagueness?
3. **Change one thing in the environment, not the intention.** A new time, a new location, a smaller commitment, a scheduled partner.
4. **Reduce the commitment until it is impossible to fail.** One 15-minute session is better than a skipped 90-minute one.
5. **Track the streak for two weeks.** Restart if needed.
6. **Return to the full protocol only after the habit is stable.**

### When to Consider Stopping

If habit failure persists for more than 4 weeks despite environmental changes, consider:

- Whether the course fits your current life
- Whether you need to use [[#Appendix O — Working Across Intensity Levels|Appendix O]]
- Whether the underlying issue is burnout, not discipline
- Whether you need external support (coach, peer, therapist)

> [!warning] Habit Failure Is Not a Moral Failing
> It is a signal. The signal usually says something about structure, not character.

---

## Failure Type 3 — Motivational Failure

**What it is:** You are doing the practice, but you no longer care why.

**Diagnosis:** This is not a habit problem or a work problem. It is an authorship problem. The work has drifted away from what you actually care about.

### Symptoms

- Going through the motions
- Producing competent work without investment
- Avoiding the standing inquiry
- Losing interest in your own premises
- Envying other people's work without knowing why

### Motivational Failure Protocol

1. **Stop producing for one week.** Continue non-instrumental attention only.
2. **Re-read your standing inquiry entries.** Look for the drift.
3. **Answer honestly:** What did I choose this week that I would choose again if nobody required me to choose it?
4. **Identify the borrowed ambition.** Whose approval are you working toward?
5. **Return to the problem-finding log.** Which problems from the past 3 months still hold your attention?
6. **Rebuild from what actually holds your attention,** not from what you think should.

> [!important] Motivational Failure Is Often a Signal That You Are Working on Problems That Are Not Yours.

### Long-Term Protocol

If motivational failure recurs:

- Annual standing inquiry becomes more important
- Consider a self-originated project with no external audience
- Revisit the difference between what you say you care about and what your practice repeatedly selects

---

## Failure Type 4 — Log Decay

**What it is:** You are doing the sessions. The logs are being filled. But the logs have stopped carrying information.

**Diagnosis:** This is neither habit failure nor motivational failure. It is a failure of **substance inside the form**. The practice is happening, but the reflection has become perfunctory. The logs are being maintained as a ritual rather than as a thinking tool.

### Symptoms

Look for these patterns in your own logs:

| Symptom | What it indicates |
|---|---|
| Every calibration confidence is "70%" | You have stopped thinking about confidence |
| Every problem-finding "stakes" field says the same thing | You have stopped thinking about stakes |
| Decision log entries describe only what you did, not what you rejected | You have stopped recording alternatives |
| Practice log "next session focus" repeats a previous entry | You have stopped planning |
| Provenance records list "human: 50%, tool: 50%" without specifics | You have stopped thinking about contribution |
| Critique record "decision" is always "apply" | You have stopped diagnosing feedback |
| Non-instrumental attention entries are vague ("saw some things") | The sessions have become checkbox activities |
| Standing inquiry entries are one-word answers | The inquiry has become a formality |

### Why This Is Distinct from Other Failures

- **Not habit failure** — the sessions are happening.
- **Not motivational failure** — you still care about the work.
- **Not output failure** — the work may be fine.

Log decay is the *interior* of the practice hollowing out while the *exterior* continues.

### Log Decay Protocol

1. **Read the last 4 weeks of one log straight through.** Do not skip entries.
2. **Look for repetition, vagueness, and default values.** Mark them.
3. **Ask for one entry:** What did I actually think here? If you cannot answer, the entry was hollow.
4. **Change one field of the log.** Make it more specific. Make it harder to fill in without thinking.
5. **Re-read the template you are using.** Is it asking the right question for where you actually are in the course?
6. **Consider whether the log has served its purpose.** Some logs are for early parts and become unnecessary later. Retiring a log is not the same as abandoning a practice.

### Field Audits

> [!important] Audits Are Not Policing
> A quarterly audit is a way to notice when a log has become a habit rather than a tool.

Every quarter, audit one log:

| Field | Question |
|---|---|
| Decision log | When did I last record a genuinely difficult decision? |
| Calibration log | When did I last record a confidence below 50% or above 90%? |
| Problem-finding log | When did I last select a problem different from what I usually choose? |
| Non-instrumental log | When did I last encounter something truly unexpected? |
| Provenance records | When did I last record a contribution that surprised me? |
| Critique records | When did I last decide to reject feedback? |

If the answer is "never," the log has drifted.

### What to Do When a Log Decays

1. **Do not restart from zero.** Keep the existing log.
2. **Add one new field** that forces new thinking.
3. **Set a specific question** you must answer at least once in the next two weeks — e.g., "Record a decision where I genuinely did not know what to do."
4. **Review after two weeks.** Did the log become useful again?
5. **If not, retire the log and replace it with a different practice.** Not every log needs to live forever.

### Prevention

Log decay is easier to prevent than to fix.

- **Rotate one field of the log each month.** Small variation prevents ritualisation.
- **Share one log entry with a peer each month.** Writing for an audience changes what gets written.
- **Read the log with delay.** Reading a month-old entry reveals whether it was thinking or filling space.
- **Never automate log entry.** A log written by a tool is not a log.

# When to Stop

[[#Table of Contents|↑ Back to TOC]]

> [!important] The Course Does Not Address This. It Should.
> Most creative training tells you how to start, build, and revise. It does not tell you how to know when a work is done, when to abandon a project, or when to stop practising for the day. This section fills that gap.

## When a Work Is Done

A work is done when:

- it satisfies its acceptance criteria
- further revision would not meaningfully improve it
- you can defend it without explaining what it is *trying* to do
- you can look at it without wanting to change it
- you have lost the ability to see it clearly because you have been inside it too long

> [!warning] The Last One Is a Trap
> If you have been inside a work so long that you cannot see it, the work may be done — or you may need distance. Wait a week. Look again. If it still looks done, it is done. If you see new problems, it is not.

## When to Abandon a Project

A project should be abandoned when:

- the premise no longer holds your attention
- the problem was not worth pursuing
- you have re-briefed more than twice and it still is not right
- the work is not teaching you anything
- you are making it only because you started it

> [!important] Abandonment Is Not Failure
> It is a decision. The decision may be correct or incorrect, but it is not a moral failing. Some of the best work comes from abandoning the wrong project early.

## When to Stop for the Day

Stop when:

- you are making changes without knowing why
- you are going in circles
- you are tired
- you are hungry
- you are angry
- you cannot remember what you were trying to do

> [!tip] The Best Time to Stop Is When You Know What to Do Next
> Hemingway's advice: stop while you still know what happens next. That way, when you return, you have a place to begin. The same applies to visual work, pipeline work, and writing. Stop at a point where the next step is clear. Do not stop at a point where you are lost.

## When to Stop the Course

The course ends when:

- you have completed the 12-week intensive
- you have completed 6 months of independent practice
- you can do the Blank-Sheet Test
- you can find a problem when nobody assigns one
- you can take responsibility for the decision to pursue it

> [!question] The Final Question
> **If the course disappeared tomorrow, would I still know how to begin?**

If the answer is yes, the course is done. If the answer is no, the course has identified what you need to practice next. Both are useful outcomes.

# Exit Criteria

[[#Table of Contents|↑ Back to TOC]]

The independent practice ends when you can independently:

1. Encounter an ambiguous situation and identify several plausible creative problems.
2. Choose one problem worth pursuing and explain why.
3. **Run a positionality check and refuse a problem you have no standing to pursue.**
4. Generate and compare premises.
5. Write a working brief without being given a template.
6. Choose an appropriate medium and production method.
7. Build or adapt a pipeline.
8. Diagnose failures at the correct level.
9. Revise or re-brief deliberately.
10. Use external feedback without outsourcing judgment.
11. Reject outputs for explicit reasons.
12. Defend decisions and refusals.
13. Produce work that demonstrates a coherent position.
14. Explain what the work is for.
15. Start another unfamiliar project without waiting for an exercise to be assigned.
16. Design your own practice plan and maintain it without supervision.
17. **Locate a problem worth pursuing when nobody has assigned one, and take responsibility for the decision to pursue it.**
18. **Sustain non-instrumental attention without converting it immediately into productivity.**
19. **Predict outcomes of system changes before running them, with calibrated confidence, and revise internal models based on the gap.**
20. **Detect when calibration has become narrowness, and widen inputs when needed.**
21. **(when collaborating) Share authorship with an equal, including economic terms, without defaulting to deference or dominance.**
22. **(when teaching) Scaffold someone else's problem-finding without supplying the problem, and get feedback on the teaching itself.**
23. **Synthesise visual and writing into one authored object when the work requires it.**
24. **Distinguish output failure, habit failure, motivational failure, and log decay — and respond to each appropriately.**
25. **Maintain physical sustainability across the craft hours.**
26. **Know when a work is done, when to abandon a project, and when to stop for the day.**

## Final Evidence

The final capstone must contain a section titled:

> **How I decided what to make.**

It should reconstruct the project from problem discovery through delivery.

The answer should demonstrate that you authored the problem, not only the solution.

> [!example] What "How I Decided What to Make" Looks Like
>
> I did not begin with a problem. I began with a situation: a city I had never visited, three days, a camera, a notebook.
>
> I spent the first day observing. I walked. I sat. I noticed. I did not try to find a problem. I let the problems find me.
>
> By the second day, I had noticed something: the city's public stairways were being used as social spaces. People gathered on them. They sat, talked, ate, performed. The stairways were designed for movement, but they were being used for staying.
>
> I framed this as a problem: what happens when a space designed for one purpose is used for another?
>
> I ran a positionality check. I was not from this city. I was an observer. I had standing to document what I saw, but not to speak for the people I saw. I decided to photograph the stairways without the people — the absence was the point. The stairways had to speak for themselves.
>
> I wrote a brief. I chose the medium (photography). I chose the constraints (no people, five images, the stairway as subject). I made the work.
>
> I diagnosed it. The first set was too pretty. The stairways looked like architecture, not social space. I re-briefed. I added a constraint: each image had to include evidence of use — a discarded cup, a chalk mark, a worn patch on the stone.
>
> The second set was better. The evidence made the absence visible. The stairways were not empty. They were full of the traces of people who were not there.
>
> I defended the work. I explained why I chose the problem, why I chose the constraints, why I rejected the first set, and what I learned. I took responsibility for the decision to pursue this problem and for the effects of representing a place I was not from.
>
> That is how I decided what to make.

# Appendix E — Anchor Library Protocol

[[#Table of Contents|↑ Back to TOC]]

## Purpose

The anchor library is the external memory of evolving judgment.

> [!important] It Is Not a Mood Board. It Is Not a Collection of Admiration. It Is a Record of **Decisions Worth Remembering**.

## Anchor Record

Each record contains:

- artifact
- source / provenance
- medium
- intended function
- what succeeds
- what fails
- mechanism
- diagnostic level
- reference class
- purpose
- accept / reject status
- date added
- date revisited
- whether criteria changed

## Comparative Judgment

Every major addition should be compared to at least two existing anchors.

## Human-Anchor Requirement

At least 40% of accepted anchors should come from human-created work, not because human work is intrinsically superior, but because you need access to decisions made under conditions not identical to the model's defaults.

## Demotion

An anchor can be demoted when:

- you discover a stronger example
- the original judgment depended on novelty
- the mechanism is inappropriate to the current purpose
- the artifact is attractive but conceptually thin
- you realise the criterion was poorly formulated

> [!tip] Demotion Is Progress.

## Versioning

Save snapshots of the library at regular intervals.

Compare old criteria against new criteria.

The evolution is part of the course.

## Range Audit

Every quarter, ask whether the library is widening or narrowing.

- Are new anchors structurally different from old ones?
- Are you adding anchors that surprise you?
- Are you demoting anchors because your criteria changed, or because you stopped thinking about them?

> [!warning] A Library That Stops Surprising You Has Stopped Calibrating You.

# Appendix F — Ethical, Technical & Intentional Refusal

[[#Table of Contents|↑ Back to TOC]]

> [!important] Refusal Is Part of Direction Because a Creative Director Is Not Merely an Acceptance Mechanism.

## 1. Ethical Refusal

The requested output should not be produced or used because doing so crosses a stated ethical boundary.

Name the principle, not simply discomfort.

## 2. Positional Refusal

The problem is one you do not have standing to pursue. See [[#Module 2 — Problem-Finding|Module 2, Positionality]].

## 3. Technical Refusal

The requested outcome cannot be reliably achieved under the available constraints.

State:

- what is technically blocking it
- what alternatives exist
- which trade-off is being accepted

## 4. Intentional Refusal

The output may be technically possible and ethically permissible but would damage the premise, voice, or visual logic of the work.

This is a particularly important form of authorship.

## Refusal Procedure

1. Restate the request.
2. Identify the underlying desired outcome.
3. Diagnose the conflict.
4. State the principle.
5. Offer an alternative when appropriate.
6. Record the decision.

## Slop Refusal

A practitioner may also reject work because it is generic in a way that undermines the project's intention.

Distinguish:

> "I don't like this"

from:

> "This fails the project's defined criteria for specificity / focal hierarchy / voice / argument / distinctiveness."

> [!example] What a Refusal Record Looks Like
>
> **Request:** Use a generated image of a real person's face in a commercial context without their consent.
>
> **Underlying outcome:** The client wants a recognizable face to lend credibility to the product.
>
> **Conflict:** Using a real person's likeness without consent violates their autonomy and may violate law.
>
> **Principle:** Do not generate identifiable people without consent.
>
> **Alternative:** Use a generated face that does not resemble any real person, or hire a model and obtain consent, or use a non-human image.
>
> **Decision:** Refuse the specific request. Offer the alternatives.
>
> **Result:** The client chose a generated face that did not resemble any real person. The campaign ran. The ethical boundary held.

# Appendix G — Transfer Tests

[[#Table of Contents|↑ Back to TOC]]

> [!important] Transfer Is Assessed Separately Because You Can Score Well on Rehearsed Exercises Without Developing Independence.

## Transfer Test 1 — New Medium

Apply diagnosis to an unfamiliar medium.

## Transfer Test 2 — New Premise

Generate and specify a premise without a worked example.

## Transfer Test 3 — New Tool

Perform the underlying operation in a different tool or manually.

## Transfer Test 4 — New Audience

Re-specify the same premise for an audience with different needs.

## Transfer Test 5 — Adversarial Critique

Defend a decision when the critic proposes a plausible but harmful alternative.

## Transfer Test 6 — Reversal

Defend an output you originally rejected by constructing a context in which it becomes correct.

## Transfer Test 7 — Blank Sheet

Start from an unfamiliar situation and construct the entire creative problem.

## Transfer Test 8 — No Assignment

Place yourself in a situation with no brief, no prompt, no deadline, and no audience. Within 90 minutes, identify a problem worth pursuing, state why it matters, and begin work.

## Transfer Test 9 — Responsibility

Take a piece of your own work and articulate what you are responsible for: selection, framing, execution, effects, disclosure, downstream use. Defend that responsibility under questioning.

## Transfer Test 10 — Calibration

Given an unfamiliar system, predict outcomes with stated confidence before running it. Then compare.

## Transfer Test 11 — Cross-Medium Synthesis

Given a premise that neither visual nor writing can carry alone, construct a synthesis solution.

## Transfer Test 12 — Collaborative Direction

Given a hypothetical or real disagreement with a co-creator, produce a written resolution that names both positions, identifies the mechanism of disagreement, and records what is lost by each outcome.

## Transfer Test 13 — Collaborative Economics

Given a hypothetical collaboration with unequal contributions, propose a revenue split, credit order, process-ownership arrangement, and dissolution clause — with reasoning.

## Transfer Test 14 — Transmission

Given a person stuck at a specific point in the creative loop, produce a diagnosis of their bottleneck, a scaffold that does not supply the answer, and a question you would ask instead of giving direction.

## Transfer Test 15 — Teaching Feedback

Take a recording of a scaffolding session. From the recording alone, identify where you supplied an answer, where you opened a question, where you filled silence, and what the learner actually decided. Compare against the learner's memory.

## Transfer Test 16 — Positionality

Given a problem that has tension, stakes, and generativity, run the positionality check. Either justify your standing or refuse the problem.

## Transfer Test 17 — Log Integrity

Read the last 4 weeks of one of your own logs. Identify the entries that are hollow. Propose one field change that would prevent recurrence.

## Transfer Scoring

A transfer test should record:

- prompt / situation
- what you did without scaffolding
- where you hesitated
- whether you imported an earlier template blindly
- what principle transferred
- what new principle had to be invented

# Appendix H — Study Protocols for Building Creative Knowledge

[[#Table of Contents|↑ Back to TOC]]

> [!note] The Course Assumes That Judgment Needs a Growing Internal Reference System.

## Visual Study Protocol 1 — Master Decomposition

1. Select a strong image.
2. Reduce to large masses.
3. Identify focal hierarchy.
4. Analyse value structure.
5. Trace directional forces.
6. Reconstruct underlying spatial forms.
7. Reproduce the logic from memory.
8. Compare.
9. Record what was missed.
10. Apply one discovered principle to a new image.

## Visual Study Protocol 2 — Transformation

1. Start with an observed object or figure.
2. Construct it simply.
3. Change viewpoint.
4. Change proportion.
5. Change gesture.
6. Preserve identity.
7. Invent an environment.
8. Compare invented result with the original logic.

The purpose is to move from observation to controlled invention.

## Visual Study Protocol 3 — Composition Extraction

For a master image:

- ignore subject identity
- identify masses
- identify directional flow
- identify contrast hierarchy
- reconstruct using unrelated subject matter

This teaches mechanisms rather than imitation.

## Visual Study Protocol 4 — Deliberate Deviation

1. Select a convention (rule of thirds, symmetry, tonal contrast, edge hierarchy).
2. State what the convention accomplishes.
3. Produce three studies that violate it deliberately.
4. For each, state what the violation achieves.
5. Decide whether any of the violations serves a purpose you would pursue.

## Writing Study Protocol 1 — Imitation

1. Select a strong passage.
2. Identify its structural moves.
3. Rewrite the passage on a different subject while preserving the structural behaviour.
4. Rewrite again without the original syntax.
5. Compare.

## Writing Study Protocol 2 — Compression

1. Take 500 words.
2. Reduce to 250.
3. Reduce to 100.
4. Reduce to 30.
5. State what was lost at each stage.
6. Restore only what serves the premise.

## Writing Study Protocol 3 — Voice Separation

Write the same claim in:

- your default voice
- a neutral professional voice
- a deliberately constrained voice
- a voice derived from an analysed human anchor

Then identify which mechanisms changed.

The purpose is to make voice controllable without reducing it to adjectives.

## Writing Study Protocol 4 — Deliberate Deviation

1. Select a convention (paragraph length, syntactic parallelism, resolution, closure).
2. State what the convention accomplishes.
3. Produce three short pieces that violate it deliberately.
4. For each, state what the violation achieves.
5. Decide whether any of the violations serves a purpose you would pursue.

## Time-Based Study Protocol (Deepening)

1. Select a short sequence (film scene, audio passage, performance fragment).
2. Analyse pacing, silence, held frames, transitions.
3. Reconstruct the logic.
4. Produce a short sequence with the same durational logic, different content.
5. Compare.

# Appendix J — Feedback, Critique & Self-Calibration

[[#Table of Contents|↑ Back to TOC]]

> [!important] Without an Instructor, You Must Become a Reliable Feedback Processor.

## Giving Critique

When giving feedback to a peer:

1. Describe what you observe before evaluating.
2. Ask what the work is trying to do before assuming.
3. Locate the problem at the correct diagnostic level.
4. Separate preference from principle.
5. Offer the smallest meaningful intervention, not a redesign.
6. State what evidence would show the intervention worked.

## Receiving Critique

1. Listen without defending.
2. Record the feedback verbatim.
3. Wait 24 hours before deciding.
4. Diagnose the underlying problem the feedback points to.
5. Decide: apply, adapt, reject, or re-brief.
6. Record the decision and reasoning.

## Self-Critique Protocol

When no external critique is available:

1. Wait 48 hours after finishing.
2. Describe the work without evaluative adjectives.
3. State what you intended.
4. State what the work actually does.
5. Identify the highest unresolved level.
6. Name the decision that created the problem.
7. Propose one intervention.
8. Test it.
9. Compare.

> [!tip] Record Yourself Describing the Work. The Hesitation Is Diagnostic.

## Calibration Practice

| Practice | Frequency | Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| Blind self-assessment | Monthly | Compare in-the-moment vs delayed judgment |
| Calibration log | Weekly | Track whether you can predict outcomes with calibrated confidence |
| Range audit | Quarterly | Detect whether calibration has become narrowness |
| Peer score comparison | Monthly | Compare your assessment against others' |
| Outcome review | Monthly | Did the work achieve its stated intention? |
| Public response check | Quarterly | Did the audience respond as intended? |

> [!warning] If Your Self-Scores Are Consistently Higher Than External Evidence Suggests, You Have a Calibration Problem. If Consistently Lower, You Have a Confidence Problem. Both Are Diagnosable and Fixable.

## Feedback Record Format

| Field | Content |
|---|---|
| Source | Who said it |
| Role | Audience / craft / domain / ordinary perception / peer |
| Feedback | What they said |
| Inferred problem | What underlying issue it points to |
| Maps to brief? | Yes / No / Partially |
| Blind spot? | What I could not see |
| Decision | Apply / Adapt / Reject / Re-brief |
| Reasoning | Why |
| Result | What happened |

# Appendix K — Safety, Ethics, Access & Sustainability

[[#Table of Contents|↑ Back to TOC]]

## Consent and Likeness

- Do not generate identifiable people without consent.
- Do not use someone's likeness for commercial or deceptive purposes.
- Understand the difference between public figure, private individual, and fictional character.
- When in doubt, do not.

## Copyright and Fair Use

- Know the license of every reference you use.
- Do not reproduce copyrighted work as your own.
- Understand the difference between study, homage, parody, and theft.
- Model training data is contested. Do not assume output is free of copyright risk.

## AI Disclosure

> [!important] Disclosure Follows the Applicable Law, Institutional Policy, Contract, Publication Standard, Competition Rules, and Materiality of the AI Contribution.

Do not treat disclosure as a universal constant. Treat it as a decision determined by context.

## Dataset Bias

- Models reflect and amplify biases in their training data.
- Check for representational bias in outputs.
- Do not assume neutrality.

## Labor and Economic Impact

- Be aware of how AI use affects other practitioners.
- Do not undercut human workers by offering AI-generated work at unsustainable prices.
- Consider the ethical dimensions of replacing human labor.

> [!important] A Note on the Broader Politics of AI
> This course is a practical training in creative judgment. It is not a political economy of AI. But a practitioner who never thinks about labor displacement, concentrated power, environmental cost, and misinformation is not fully equipped. The course points at these questions. It does not answer them. You should.

## Environmental Cost

- Generation has compute cost.
- Batch where possible.
- Do not generate wastefully.
- Consider whether a manual solution would be sufficient.

## Accessibility

- Ensure your outputs are accessible: alt text, captions, readable typography, contrast.
- Consider how your work reaches people with different sensory, cognitive, and physical needs.

## Neurodiversity and Disability

- Adapt the course to your needs.
- The practice structure can be modified for energy, focus, and sensory constraints.
- Rest is part of practice.

## Language and Cultural Context

- Be aware of cultural references you may not fully understand.
- Do not use culturally specific imagery without context or permission.
- Translate assumptions, not just words.

## Data Security

- Do not upload confidential client material to tools that train on inputs.
- Use encrypted storage for sensitive work.
- Understand where your data goes.

## Mental Health

- Comparison pressure is real.
- Portfolio anxiety is real.
- AI-generated work can produce impostor feelings.
- Rest is part of the practice.
- If practice becomes harmful, stop and reassess.
- See [[#Failure Protocols|Failure Type 3]] for the protocol on motivational failure.

## Physical Sustainability

> [!important] The Craft Hours Have a Body Cost
> See [[#Craft Knowledge Requirement|Craft Knowledge Requirement, Physical sustainability]] for the full protocol.

- Break every 30–45 min during drawing / tablet work.
- Neutral wrist and elbow position during drawing / typing.
- Screen distance at arm's length or more.
- 20-20-20 rule during screen-based work.
- Daily non-screen movement.
- Sleep discipline.

### Warning Signs

Stop and reassess if you experience:

- persistent wrist, hand, or forearm pain
- numbness or tingling in fingers
- recurring headaches during or after screen work
- eye strain that does not resolve with rest
- shoulder or neck pain that persists across days
- loss of grip strength or fine motor control

# Appendix M — Professional Practice Reference

[[#Table of Contents|↑ Back to TOC]]

> [!note] This Appendix Is a Reference Manual. Professional Practice Begins in [[#Module 13 — Professional Practice Begins|Module 13]] and Is Embedded in Part V, Part VI, and Independent Practice.

## Contracts and IP

- Know who owns the work you produce.
- Understand work-for-hire vs license vs assignment.
- Do not sign away rights you need.
- Get it in writing.

### AI-Specific Contract Questions

- Are AI-generated elements permitted under the client's contract?
- Who is responsible for model / tool licensing compliance?
- Are third-party reference images permitted?
- Who warrants originality?
- Who bears responsibility for infringement claims?
- Can the client use outputs for training or further generation?
- Are prompts, workflows, LoRAs, source files, or datasets included in delivery?
- What happens if a tool changes its terms after delivery?
- Are disclosure requirements the client's responsibility or the creator's?

### Pipeline-as-IP

> [!important] The Deliverable Is Not Only the Final Artifact
> In an AI-augmented practice, what you deliver may include:
>
> **brief + decision system + pipeline + source material + output + rights**

This means contracts should specify what happens to the *system*, not only the *result*.

A pipeline may be more valuable than any single output. Treat it as valuable know-how.

> [!warning] Not All Know-How Is Legally Protectable IP
> Distinguish between:
> - **Commercially valuable know-how** — your methods, taste, decision frameworks, and process knowledge. Often valuable but not necessarily protected by IP law.
> - **Legally protected IP** — copyright, trademark, patent, trade secret, design rights. These require specific legal conditions to apply.
>
> The course uses "pipeline-as-IP" as a commercial concept, not a legal claim. Consult a lawyer before assuming any specific protection.

## Pricing

> [!important] Price the Decision-Making, Not Just the Pixels or Words
> AI-augmented production can make raw generation cost very low. The decision-making and responsibility remain expensive.

Pricing should account for:

- **Creative judgment** — the problem-finding, premise, and direction that shape the work
- **Production** — generation, drafting, rendering, editing
- **Revisions** — the number of cycles, their scope, their cost
- **Communication** — meetings, feedback diagnosis, stakeholder alignment
- **Administration** — project management, invoicing, record-keeping
- **Risk** — uncertainty, scope creep, contested originality, licensing
- **Usage rights** — how the work will be used, for how long, in what territories, for what purposes

Price by value, but define value as this composite — not a vague sense of "quality."

## Scope

- Define what is included.
- Define what is not included.
- Define revision limits.
- Define what triggers a re-brief (and additional cost).

## Client Communication

- Diagnose the request before responding.
- Separate the stated problem from the actual problem.
- Ask what outcome the client is trying to change.
- Propose alternatives rather than simply refusing.
- Document decisions.

## Negotiation

- Know your walk-away point.
- Understand the client's constraints.
- Offer options, not ultimatums.
- Be willing to refuse.

## Portfolio Ethics

- Do not claim work you did not do.
- Disclose AI involvement where required by the applicable standard.
- Credit collaborators.
- Do not use client work without permission.

## Disclosure Norms

> [!important] Disclosure Follows the Applicable Law, Institutional Policy, Contract, Publication Standard, Competition Rules, and Materiality of the AI Contribution.
> Not a universal constant. Determine it per context.

| Context | Determine disclosure from |
|---|---|
| Fine art | Gallery / institution policy, medium, audience expectations |
| Commercial | Client contract, platform policy, representation of authorship |
| Journalism | Publication standards and editorial policy |
| Academic | Institutional and course policy |
| Competitions | Submission rules |
| Social media | Platform rules + audience / context |
| Client work | Contract terms + the client's own disclosure obligations |
| Public or civic work | Applicable law + public interest considerations |

The durable question is not "Do I always disclose?" but:

> **Given the applicable standards and the materiality of the AI contribution, what disclosure is required, expected, and honest?**

## Collaboration and Authorship

- Negotiate authorship before starting.
- Write decision rights explicitly.
- Record disagreements, not only agreements.
- Agree on exit terms before you need them.
- Credit collaborators even when the work is technically yours.
- Agree on economics before the first deliverable.

# Appendix N — Continuing Practice After Exit

> [!important] The Course Ends When External Structure Becomes Unnecessary. The Practice Begins When Self-Direction Becomes Possible.

Without a maintenance structure, skills decay and judgment drifts.

## Weekly

- 1 deliberate practice block (60–90 min)
- 1 project work session
- 1 standing inquiry entry
- 1 problem-finding rep (informal, 15 min)
- 1 protected non-instrumental session (30–60 min)
- 1 play session (20–30 min)

## Monthly

- 1 **problem-finding session** (60–90 min)
- 1 **unassigned beginning** (60 min)
- 1 peer critique session
- 1 decision log review
- 1 practice log review
- 1 new anchor addition and 1 demotion review
- 1 calibration review

## Quarterly

- 1 portfolio review
- 1 position review
- 1 tool refresh (check for changes)
- 1 blind self-assessment
- 1 range audit (is my taste widening or narrowing?)
- 1 log integrity audit (see [[#Failure Protocols|Failure Type 4]])

## Annually

- 1 full portfolio review
- 1 authorial position synthesis
- 1 skill decay check (test a skill you have not used)
- 1 new-domain transfer project
- 1 practice plan revision
- 1 responsibility review
- 1 physical sustainability audit

## The Recurring Problem-Finding Session

> [!important] The Maintenance System Must Exercise the Upstream Faculty, Not Only the Downstream One
> See full protocol in [[#The Monthly Problem-Finding Session|The Monthly Problem-Finding Session]].

Once a month, before choosing a project:

> Spend 60–90 minutes identifying something worth pursuing without an assigned brief.

Record:

**Observation → Question → Tension → Mode → Positionality → Possible premise → Stakes**

Do not necessarily produce the work.

The objective is to keep exercising the **upstream creative faculty**, not merely production.

Otherwise you can become very good at executing independently supplied ideas while believing you have achieved independence.

Store entries in the **Problem-Finding Log**. Over time, this log becomes a record of your independent creative attention — and a source of future projects.

## The Recurring Unassigned Beginning

> [!important] One Unassigned Beginning per Month
> Start with no brief, no target deliverable, and no predetermined medium. Spend 60 minutes finding something worth pursuing. Then stop.

Why stop?

Because otherwise you can become very disciplined about **doing projects** while slowly losing the ability to **notice projects**.

The course understands this distinction. The maintenance system should preserve it indefinitely.

Store entries in a dedicated section of the **Problem-Finding Log**.

## Protected Non-Instrumental Attention

> [!important] One Session per Week
> 30–60 minutes of attention with no problem to solve, no output requirement, no evaluation, and no optimisation.

Just looking, reading, walking, collecting, sketching, noticing.

Afterward, write one line:

> What did I encounter that I was not looking for?

Do not analyse it. Do not integrate it.

This practice preserves the space where serendipity operates. Without it, every act of perception is immediately converted into productivity — and the material that makes work distinctive never surfaces.

## Play Maintenance

> [!important] One Play Session per Week
> 20–30 minutes of unstructured making for its own sake. No brief, no deliverable, no diagnosis, no criteria, no audience.

Afterward, write one line:

> What did I enjoy?

Do not analyse it. Do not integrate it.

Play preserves the space where the unexpected, the silly, the joyful, and the genuinely new can appear. Without it, the practice narrows to what you already know how to do.

## Skill Decay Checks

Pick a skill you have not practised in 3 months. Test it.

If it has decayed, schedule a refresher block.

If it has not, note why (maybe it transferred to a more general capacity).

## Tool Refresh Cycle

| Frequency | Action |
|---|---|
| Monthly | Check for major model updates |
| Quarterly | Test one new tool against your current stack |
| Annually | Re-evaluate your entire tool stack against your practice goals |

## New-Domain Transfer Project

Once a year, apply your full creative loop to a domain you have never worked in.

This is the strongest test of whether your competence is genuinely transferable or merely domain-specific.

## Annual Responsibility Review

Once a year, review the work you produced.

For each major piece, answer:

- What problem was I pursuing?
- Why did I choose it?
- Who was affected by it?
- What did I disclose?
- What am I responsible for?

This is the maintenance rehearsal for the third layer of mastery: **agency**.

## Collaboration Maintenance

If your practice includes ongoing collaborations:

- Review authorship agreements annually.
- Review decision rights when the project changes.
- Review economics agreements when the project scales.
- Re-read the conflict log from the past year.
- Note what resolved well and what did not.

# Appendix O — Working Across Intensity Levels

[[#Table of Contents|↑ Back to TOC]]

> [!important] The Intensive Is Designed for 18–25 hrs/week (Core) or 25–35 hrs/week (with Deepening)
> Neither is compatible with full-time employment without adjustment. This appendix preserves the scaffolding sequence at reduced intensity.

## The Principle

The course is a sequence of scaffolding removals. What matters is **the sequence**, not the calendar.

Slower is fine. Skipping is not.

## Three Intensity Paths

| Path | Hours/week | Duration | Who it suits |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Full-time intensive** | 25–35 | 12 weeks | Learners with funded time |
| **Extended intensive** | 12–18 | 24 weeks | Learners with part-time work |
| **Distributed practice** | 6–10 | 48 weeks | Learners with full-time work |

The **same sequence** of parts and scaffolding removals applies to all three paths.

## What Changes with Intensity

| Element | Full-time | Extended | Distributed |
|---|---|---|---|
| Weekly practice blocks | 3× | 2× | 1× |
| Visual craft study | 4× weekly | 2× weekly | 1× weekly |
| Writing craft study | 4× weekly | 2× weekly | 1× weekly |
| Project application | 2–3 hrs | 1–2 hrs | 45 min |
| Transfer test | weekly | weekly | biweekly |
| Non-instrumental attention | weekly | weekly | weekly |
| Play | weekly | weekly | weekly |
| Problem-finding | weekly | weekly | monthly |
| Critique | weekly | biweekly | monthly |

## What Does Not Change

- The scaffolding sequence
- The problem-finding log
- The non-instrumental attention practice
- The play practice
- The calibration log
- The baseline and practice contract
- The transfer tests
- The final Blank-Sheet Test

## Distributed Practice Rhythm

A typical distributed week (6–10 hrs/week):

| Day | Activity | Time |
|---|---|---|
| Mon | Core concept + problem-finding rep | 45 min |
| Tue | Visual craft study | 60 min |
| Wed | Writing craft study | 60 min |
| Thu | Project application | 60 min |
| Fri | Practice drill | 60 min |
| Sat | Non-instrumental attention + play | 60 min |
| Sun | Log review + standing inquiry | 30 min |

Total: ~6–7 hrs/week.

> [!warning] There Is a Maintenance Threshold
> Below ~6 hrs/week, the course becomes **maintenance** rather than a course path. It is possible to keep practising at ~4 hrs/week, but at that intensity the scaffolding sequence breaks down: parts cannot be completed in the intended order, transfer tests become sporadic, and the final Blank-Sheet Test cannot be meaningfully prepared for.
>
> **6–10 hrs/week = distributed course.**
> **~4 hrs/week = maintenance / minimum practice, not a full course path.**

If you are running at ~4 hrs/week, be honest about what you are doing: maintaining practice, not completing the course. That is a valid choice. It is not the same as the distributed path.

## What Full-Time Learners Should Not Do

Do not attempt all deepening options simultaneously. The Core spine is the course. Deepening is depth, not coverage.

## What Distributed Learners Should Not Do

Do not skip the non-instrumental attention session because hours are limited. It is the most easily sacrificed and the most necessary.

Do not skip the play session. It is the space where the unexpected appears.

Do not skip the problem-finding session. It is the faculty you are trying to preserve.

## When Intensity Needs to Change Mid-Course

If you begin full-time and need to reduce:

1. Stop new project work.
2. Keep the practice log, problem-finding log, and non-instrumental attention active.
3. Resume at the next part boundary, not mid-part.
4. Do not skip parts.

If you begin distributed and can accelerate:

1. Do not skip parts.
2. Add deepening options, not new parts.
3. Complete the current part before accelerating.

## The Income Question

Full-time intensive requires funding. See [[#Week 0 — Baseline & Practice Contract|Week 0, Section 3]].

If funding is not available, choose extended or distributed practice. Do not attempt the full-time intensive without runway.

> [!warning] The Most Common Failure Mode
> Learners attempt the full-time intensive while working full-time. The result is habit failure (see [[#Failure Protocols|Failure Type 2]]), which is not a discipline problem — it is an intensity problem.

# Closing

[[#Table of Contents|↑ Back to TOC]]

## The Course in One Sentence

> [!important] **Learn to find, see, decide what is worth making, specify it, build a process, judge what returns, defend the choices, and eventually do the entire thing — including choosing the problem itself — without being told what the exercise is.**

## What This Course Is Not

- It is not a prompt-engineering course.
- It is not a software tutorial.
- It is not traditional art school compressed into twelve weeks.
- It is not an argument that hand skill no longer matters.
- It is not an argument that AI automatically makes creativity better.
- It is not a system for manufacturing a single aesthetic.
- It is not a philosophy course pretending to answer what a human life is for.
- It is not a substitute for external feedback, community, or real-world stakes.
- It is not a solitary practice only.
- It is not a course that requires teaching to be complete.
- It is not a guarantee of anything.

## What This Course Is

It is a training system for **independent creative judgment under conditions of abundant generation**.

It treats AI as a component of the creative environment rather than the centre of the course.

It treats problem-finding, positionality, collaboration, transmission, and responsibility as first-class competencies — Core when universal, Contextual when they apply.

You must become capable of:

> [!important] The Full Causal Chain
> **Finding → Seeing → Deciding → Specifying → Building → Judging → Revising → Defending → Transferring**

Which means, concretely:

- finding a problem when none is assigned
- situating yourself honestly in relation to the problem
- observing without being told what to see
- choosing without waiting for a prompt
- specifying without hiding behind vague language
- building without blindly copying workflows
- predicting outcomes before running experiments, with calibrated confidence
- curating without confusing taste with preference
- revising without endlessly patching
- refusing without defensiveness
- defending without certainty theatre
- learning without requiring the exercise to be pre-designed
- practising without waiting for an assignment
- playing without a reason
- collaborating without deference or dominance when collaboration applies
- teaching without doing the work for someone else when teaching applies
- stopping when a work is done, when a project should be abandoned, or when the day is over
- **taking responsibility for what you choose to make and why**

## The Three Layers

The deepest measure of progress is not:

> **How much can you produce?**

It is not even:

> **How well can you direct a machine?**

There are three layers, and each is stronger than the last:

**Layer 1 — Capability:** *Can I do the work?*

↓

**Layer 2 — Independence:** *Can I determine what work needs to be done?*

↓

**Layer 3 — Agency:** *Can I decide what is worth doing, and take responsibility for doing it?*

The decisive question is therefore:

> [!question] **When the machine can produce almost anything, can you determine what is worth asking it to produce — and can you tell when the answer is wrong?**

And beyond that:

> [!question] **When nobody asks you to make anything at all, can you find a problem worth pursuing — and can you take responsibility for the decision to pursue it?**

That is where the course ends.

That is where the practice begins.
